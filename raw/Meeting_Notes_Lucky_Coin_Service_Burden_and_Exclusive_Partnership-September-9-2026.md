## Summary

JVL catered lunch at the operator's shop so Val could ask the technicians directly what they hate about JVL machines, then made an exclusivity offer. Val opened by telling them they could not insult him. The technicians took him at his word.

**Lucky Coin.** A route business built on pool tables and jukeboxes rather than fast-paced amusement, with somewhere between thirteen and twenty-plus JVL machines and, by Kyle's own correction, north of forty locations. Two or three people service the video machines.

**The single worst thing: decommission and recommission.** The technicians' top complaint is having to decommission a game and flash it to fix errors that should not require it. Flashing wipes the game's history back to zero, which then causes problems with the lottery's central system. One technician avoids it wherever he can.

The worked example: a brand new game was in the field for ten days when the lottery disabled it, because the signature the game reported at commissioning was no longer the signature it was sending to the site controller. When the technician called JVL he was told this does not happen on new games while he was standing in front of it happening. A JVL colleague described the workaround — go to status, find it locked by the accounting system, unlock manually — and clarified that his "it cannot happen" meant it cannot be the wrong version, not that it was not happening. The technician said the manual unlock held for a minute and then reverted. While fixing it he turned the volume down, pressed a button inside the volume menu, and that broke the whole game, requiring a flash. At that moment the game had $2,600 of a player's credits locked on it. The fix was to decommission, flash, make the money disappear, and recommission. His bar owner and his customers were not happy. A colleague noted the accounting was saved only because the data happened to be captured before the reset.

**Flashing depends on JVL, and that is the friction.** The technician's real objection to the process is the passport step: it makes him reliant on JVL to complete a repair. With an IGT or Aristocrat game he brings his own dongle, plugs it in, and it is done — no message to send, no response to wait for. He was clear that JVL is almost always quick to respond; the problem is that it is one more person in the loop. Val asked whether automating it would be acceptable and he said yes — scan a QR code, type something in, done.

The QR code itself is unreliable today. A technician scanned through pages two, three, four and five, and at the end it said it was sent but never confirmed completion. He had to have someone check the back end to learn it had gone through, and he took the photographs about fifteen times.

**Spills kill the V-deck.** The technicians like the V-deck and say players like it, but a knocked-over drink kills the touchscreen controller board underneath and the whole deck has to be replaced. One technician has replaced three, keeps one or two spares on the shelf, and has resorted to wrapping the I/O boards in cling film so they cannot get wet, which he says helps a lot. It happens in bars, mostly where drinking at the machines is allowed, but he said anywhere he has ever put a V-deck game he has eventually had to replace the deck. A JVL colleague explained the advance-replacement arrangement — the customer fits a new set, returns the old, and JVL invoices — and confirmed it is a couple of screws and a couple of wires.

Val's answer was that this is a design trade-off, not a defect he can seal away. JVL does have internal drainage and water-tested it, but a flat surface eventually lets spillage penetrate, and even where the board and connector survive, the touchscreen is left with a sticky residue that makes the machine unpleasant to play and has to be replaced anyway. Competitors' decks are slanted so liquid cascades to the floor, but slanting removes the comfort that makes players sit and eat and drink at the machine. He noted the technicians see the same failures on IGT and Light & Wonder V-decks; Aristocrat has no V-deck at all. Val offered a button deck as an immediate swap, available today. The operator's answer: next time a V-deck fails, swap that one for a button panel and see what happens, rather than changing anything now.

**Other hardware.** The overwhelming majority of service calls are a power supply or an I/O board. The technician asked whether the board under the V-deck could be moved inside the cabinet instead. On a new cabinet delivered recently the subwoofer was loose in the bottom and not bolted down; a JVL colleague said it is never bolted, but Val overrode that — if it can come out of its tray, it is an issue.

Val declined to discuss an Altera-related problem, calling Altera thirty-year-old technology. On freezing games, the operator has smart plugs at some locations so they can power-cycle remotely from a technician's phone and watch machines drop off and return on the Interlot portal. Val said plainly he cannot fix the freeze tomorrow, that it will take time, and that buying a new machine would not change that — but that the point was well taken.

**Service load, honestly stated.** Asked to compare, the technicians said JVL machines take above-average attention: a relatively small number of units on the route absorbing a disproportionate share of their time, with more phone calls and more time per call than other manufacturers. Part of that they attributed to being less familiar with the JVL system.

**But the games earn.** Both Aaron and Kyle were clear the product is not bad — the games do well enough, they look great, and players play them when they are working. The link progressive games are performing, and the technicians said players play the progressive jackpots better than the new game JVL just released. Asked whether JVL should keep making link games, the answer was yes. A software update for the new game is pending approval; Val said call it October 1.

**Price is the real obstacle.** Kyle laid out his lens: JVL at $15,750 against a new Aristocrat at $18,000–19,000 or a new Bad Dog game at $10,000–12,000. Aristocrats he can place widely. For JVL to make sense at that price it has to work better, and today it does not make sense everywhere — it might if it were cheaper. He named a few JVL machines that do really well but called the price a hurdle he has to get over.

**Val's offer.** Val asked outright for the operator's exclusive business — all of it. In exchange: one machine per location at $15,750, five additional ports beyond the one in the machine, one IQ point-of-sale unit, and one Pay unit with screen and touchscreen, optical reader, front-load bill acceptor, ticket printer and card dispenser. He will station an engineer locally for a limited time at the start, and sweetened it with a FlexPay per location, which he priced at $7,950 — about $15,000 of equipment per location given free. He also offered to replace the operator's ten or twelve existing German or Austrian kiosks for free, and said if the JVL kiosk does not work they can bring the old ones back out and have lost nothing. Val's stated reason for asking: he is moving an engineer to the area, moving another, and is serious about the market — and, he argued, no one from IGT or Aristocrat has ever sat in their shop with sandwiches offering to design to their specification.

**Kyle's blocker.** He was warm about being listened to and called the deal a deal, but his problem is that his technicians have been through a lot of testing over the last couple of years, and if he tells them they are testing something else "they're gonna be outside my office with pitchforks and lanterns." He wanted to talk to Curtis, Aaron and the people in the room, and asked for a couple of hours. Val is travelling and back Monday night, so he asked for an answer on Tuesday. The operator agreed and called it more than fair.

**Rollout pace and the modular question.** Val asked for a machine in every location within half a year; the operator pushed back — he still has to fund the machines themselves, and fifty at sixteen thousand is real money. Val offered eight months and the operator said he was comfortable going down that road, with the detail to be settled later. On modularity, the operator wanted confirmation that the Pay unit can be opened up for a recycler if Georgia's regulations change. Val was clear it cannot: a recycler will not fit alongside the card dispenser and bill acceptor, and he does not think it will be needed. He said he believes something else will happen, declined to say what, and expects the unit to stay as it is with a second piece of equipment added beside it. An ATM would be a separate purchase, and Val does not think what is needed will be exactly an ATM.

Val closed by saying a salesperson should leave the moment the deal is reached, and left.

## Decisions Made

- **The offer: exclusive business in exchange for one machine per location at $15,750, plus five additional ports, one IQ, one Pay unit (screen and touchscreen, optical reader, front-load bill acceptor, ticket printer, card dispenser), a locally stationed engineer for a limited time, and a FlexPay per location valued at $7,950** — roughly $15,000 of free equipment per location. (Val Levitan)
- **JVL will replace the operator's ten to twelve existing German/Austrian kiosks free, with the old units available to reinstate if the JVL kiosk fails.** (Val Levitan)
- **Kyle will answer on Tuesday** — he asked for a couple of hours, Val is away until Monday night, and both settled on Tuesday. (Kyle Shepherd, agreed with Val)
- **Rollout pace: a machine in every location, half a year stretched to eight months** — Val opened at six months, Kyle pushed back on funding fifty machines, Val offered eight and said he was comfortable. Details deferred. (Val Levitan, Kyle Shepherd)
- **The button deck stays available as an immediate swap, but no swap now** — the operator will convert the next V-deck that fails and see how it goes, rather than changing anything today. This is Kyle's position, and it supersedes Val's offer to swap today. (Kyle Shepherd)
- **JVL keeps making the link progressive games** — Aaron and Curtis confirmed players play them better than the new release. (Aaron, Curtis; accepted by Val)
- **The Pay unit will not be re-opened for a recycler** — it will not fit alongside the card dispenser and bill acceptor. If regulations change, the answer is a second piece of equipment beside it, purchased separately, and Val does not expect it to be exactly an ATM. (Val Levitan)
- **The loose subwoofer is a defect** — a JVL colleague said it is never bolted down; Val overruled him. (Val Levitan)
- **The freeze problem cannot be fixed quickly** — Val stated plainly he cannot fix it tomorrow, it will take time, and a new machine would not change that. (Val Levitan)
- **The software update for the new game is targeted at October 1**, pending approval. (Val Levitan)
- **Altera-related issues are out of scope** — Val called it thirty-year-old technology and declined to go there. (Val Levitan)
