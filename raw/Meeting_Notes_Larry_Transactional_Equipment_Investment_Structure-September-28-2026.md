## Summary

Lunch meeting, English. Larry had already discussed the idea with Val on a walk and told John and Eddie the gist. The first 5 minutes are small talk and ordering.

**Georgia background (Val).** Georgia does not allow gambling; COAM machines are "skill" machines. Wins were paid as redemption tickets, which created a black market. Since July 1, wins must go on a prepaid card. About **47,000 machines in 7,400 locations**, served by about 200 operators, about 5 distributors and about 15 manufacturers. JVL is a manufacturer and cannot be vertically integrated. Most locations (about 5,500) use a free $300 handheld unit that has errors and fraud. JVL is launching point of sale, a self-checkout kiosk and AFT (temporary e-wallet as a QR ticket). Val's cost for the full set is **about $4,000 per location; retail about $15,000–16,000**. Transactional equipment is not regulated by GLC.

**JVL position.** About **2,700 JVL machines in 1,200–1,500 locations**. JVL is number 3 to 6 in Georgia. Val named two causes: a technical problem that took 1.5 years to find, and a QR/passport design that operators find annoying. About 65 employees (down from 150). Uncollected receivables under 0.5%.

**Business model (Val).** Either sell the equipment and charge $90/month, or give it free and charge **$150/month** per location, with a 5-year contract and advance replacement (about 2% replacement cost). Val's example: 7,000 locations × $4,000 = $28M; revenue about $12M/year; free cash about $10M/year; at 10× about a $100M business. Realistic target: **20% of the market, about 2,000–2,500 locations (about $8M outlay)**. Master distributors pay the monthly fee. AFTs also go into competitors' machines as open-architecture peripherals. Installations are lined up and start in October (the recording is unclear on the exact date; a partner said "seventeen days away"). Units work in the shop, not yet in a location. Val will go ahead "with or without you".

**Larry's proposed structure.**
- **Entity A** (the partners, possibly 50/50 with Val; Val offered to register "JVL Distributing" in Georgia) buys the equipment from Val's manufacturing company at $16,000: $4,000 cash up front, $12,000 as an open receivable to Val's company. Entity A takes the depreciation (about 25% more than they get now).
- **Entity B**, a joint venture of the partners and Val, runs the active business: distributes, manages and services the equipment and collects $150/month from master distributors. Part of the fee repays the $12,000 receivable through a side agreement. After expenses, a 50/50 split.
- The business must be **active, not passive**, to allow the depreciation (as with their railcar deals).
- A distributor license is needed. Val thinks about 30 days; he offered to license his second company and sell it. Larry noted licenses may not transfer, so they may apply themselves. Val agreed and said no one should invest until it is 100% clear.

**The "dream" (Val).** Val told the history with InComm (brokerage agreement; half a percent offered; the August show where GLC objected to cash-out and InComm's negotiator resigned). Georgia had about **$6.4B wagered and $5B paid out in 2025**. Prepaid-card fees could be 1–8% of payouts. Whoever owns the transactional equipment clears the transactions ("toll booth", "Trojan horse"). Val has two other offers, one a handshake giving him 50% of banking transactions. **Val and Larry both said the banking side must be treated as something that may never happen.** Larry and a partner said they would likely do the deal even without it: same cash outlay as railcars, 25% more depreciation, revenue from month one.

Other markets: Nebraska is small but new. Texas is poorly regulated (county by county); Val would not go there now.
