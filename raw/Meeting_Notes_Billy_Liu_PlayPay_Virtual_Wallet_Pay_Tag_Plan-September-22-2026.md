## Summary

**John leaving InComm.** Billy and John have been talking privately for some time. They are friends but competitors, so Billy keeps it quiet. John was unhappy at InComm and had asked Billy some weeks ago if Billy could hire him. Billy said he could not afford him. John went to Bulldog instead, because Bulldog could afford him. Billy guesses John is worth at least $300,000 a year. John ran InComm's Coin Card division from the pilot program onward. He was also angry about the Skill Pay announcement — Billy says John was irate, that InComm went against all logic and reason and pushed it through anyway, and that John is glad he left. Bulldog is large, operates in many states, and already has a closed-loop card contract, so Billy thinks John has room to work there.

Earlier, John had suggested to Billy that the two firms join forces — Billy supplying a virtual card, InComm a physical one. Billy read that as a takeover attempt, not a partnership, and did not pursue it.

**The blue card is being refused.** InComm sent a notice to stores that the Skill Pay card was coming. That made the location license holders (LLHs — the location owners) talk to each other. United, the biggest operator in the state, pulled out and said it would not go forward with the card. Billy says the others then backed away too. The fear is that InComm would disclose to the lottery which locations took the card, and those locations would be targeted. At the C-store show, owners said they are fine as they are and do not need the card.

**Fiery Pay.** Billy calls it a carbon copy — it issues a physical card through another program manager. He does not think it can go forward and does not think it wants to be the only one left doing it.

**InComm is not going away.** Billy's point is that losing the blue card does not cost InComm its existing black-card network. That business continues.

**The legal line.** Val laid out the distinction: the black card gives ATM or cash-back access, which is a direct path to cash, and the legislation makes that illegal. So it becomes the location's choice whether to break the law. Billy's view is that locations already break the law anyway and simply do not want complications. Val's framing: locations break the law quietly today; taking the blue card breaks it loudly, and openly once the transaction is facilitated on the machine.

**How Billy stays compliant.** He turns off two features. He normally allows funds to be pushed back to a bank account or out to a bank debit card. Both get switched off. What remains is the virtual wallet, whose balance can be turned into a reloadable virtual Visa.

**The anonymity problem.** Val's concern is that a player must identify himself to use a phone wallet, and an anonymous player has an advantage. Billy agrees, but thinks not for long — the physical card has existed since the 1980s and he cannot replace it, only offer his own product. He argues a phone number is normal now: no social security number is asked, and two-factor sign-up is routine even at a coffee shop rewards program.

**Physical card versus pay tag.** Val proposed issuing a physical card that is only a barcode, mirroring the virtual card, dispensed from a kiosk — an e-wallet in plastic form. Billy said that is costly, and pointed to the API they have already built: it sends a "pay tag", an alphanumeric handle the player gets on sign-up. At the kiosk the player either scans the pay tag or types it on screen, and the money goes to that person's wallet. Val agreed printing cards is costly and inefficient and said he was not arguing the point.

**The incentive plan.** Both expect players to want something in their hands at first, so cards would be dispensed early on. The plan is to offer $5 in promo credit to players who take the phone route and skip the plastic — in effect penalising the physical-card choice by $5. Val expects one player to notice, tell the others, and the physical card to fade out. The number is a placeholder; the real cost was not settled.

**Why the float matters.** Val's point: cash-back was killing the economics, because players converted to cash immediately and the money was never held. With cash-back prohibited, the balance stays on the wallet. "If we're holding a hundred million dollars, we can make a lot of money on it." Billy called it a blessing in disguise. Val added they would also have the player's phone number, and said plainly he has not yet worked out how to make money from that.

**Tone.** Val corrected Billy's phrasing. Billy said the trick is "how we con the player to give up their phone number"; Val said he did not like the choice of words and restated it as how to incentivise a player to release his phone number in exchange for marketing benefits.

**No deal exists.** Val said openly that he is discussing all of this without knowing whether he and Billy will ever make a deal.

## Decisions Made

- **Billy will switch off two features on his system** — push-back to a bank account, and push-out to a bank debit card — so the product complies. (Billy Liu)
- **The pay tag through the existing API is the preferred mechanism, not a dispensed barcode card.** Val accepted Billy's cost argument and stopped pressing the plastic-card idea. (Both)
- **Physical cards would still be dispensed at the start, with a $5 promo credit offered to players who go phone-only.** The $5 is a placeholder figure, not a settled price. (Both)

Note: these are positions agreed in conversation. There is no partnership agreement — Val said so directly.
