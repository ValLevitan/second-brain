## Summary

The third stop of Val's June route through Nebraska and South Dakota, and the only South Dakota call. MG Oil is JVL's single South Dakota customer, which shaped the whole conversation: whether JVL should keep an office in the state at all, and what to strip out of the software for a customer whose regulator forbids almost every change.

**Who MG Oil is.** A private, family-owned diversified business in Rapid City, run by Troy Erickson (60) and started by his father Martin, a Standard Oil rep who bought a small oil jobbership and began converting old service stations into convenience stores. Today: 50–60 fuel semis covering South Dakota, part of North Dakota, a little of Wyoming and Nebraska; about 40 convenience stores; two truck stops (plus more around Sioux Falls); several restaurants; a substantial rental-property and strip-mall portfolio bought up around their standalone casinos; several hundred employees. Dana's own path was the same — fifteen years managing the Flying J truck stop from 1990, noticed the lottery machines another vendor had placed there, and MG Oil started buying its own. The route grew by supplying machines to the convenience stores they already delivered fuel to, then by buying out retiring operators. Dana was blunt about the dependency: *"we wouldn't be in the business if it weren't for the lottery."* No partners, never wanted any. Troy is in the process of buying the company from his father, who is largely retired to Key West. Of Troy's two daughters, Mackenzie Erickson is rotating through the business; Tessa is not involved. Management across the group is broadly Troy's and Dana's age — Dana flagged the succession exposure himself: *"one of the things is that we're all getting older."*

**Competitive picture in South Dakota.** MG Oil and **Axcess Entertainment** are the two large operators left; Axcess is roughly 25–30% of MG Oil's size, has bought up about five other routes, and is backed by **Harms Oil** (a similar fuel company with less retail) plus two partners named Jones. Excel Gaming operates around the region but nothing in South Dakota that Dana knows of. Everyone else is a ten- or twenty-machine operator. Dana noted J&J is moving into Nebraska — Val confirmed J&J is very large there.

**Nebraska is run by TNT out of Wagner, and may be for sale.** MG Oil's Nebraska business trades as **T&T / TNT** from the Wagner, South Dakota office. It came in through an acquisition: TNT had earlier bought the small company that ran the Nebraska route, and MG Oil then bought TNT. The two people who run it are **Robert "Whitey" Tolliver** and **Mike Kotab**, both based in Wagner; the man on the ground in Nebraska reports to them. Dana was candid that Nebraska gets less attention than it deserves — *"it's a long way away from us... somebody local could do a better job"* — and that he thinks Troy would sell the Nebraska operation if the right buyer appeared. Decision-making on Nebraska sits with Troy plus Whitey and Mike.

**The regulatory wall.** South Dakota is the strictest environment JVL has certified into. Operators run exactly what the state approves and nothing else: a bill acceptor can only be replaced with the identical part, no substitute devices are permitted, and the machine's appearance is fixed — *"we painted a cabinet once and they made us destroy it."* Val recognised this from certification, where South Dakota was the only jurisdiction insisting the bill of sale name one specific validator. Dana added the layout constraint that kills kiosks: ten machines per licence, and each ten-machine room counts as a separate establishment needing its own alcohol service and its own site controller. Those controllers do not talk to each other, so a ticket from one room cannot be played in the next — even with fifty machines under one roof. MG Oil looked at a kiosk with a Nebraska company before and it went nowhere for exactly this reason. Making it work would need a rule change, and Dana's assessment of that was flat: *"we've been trying to get the [payout] limit increased for twenty years, and it goes nowhere."*

**Val's transactional pitch.** Val opened with his own credentials — Cash Code, the bill-acceptor manufacturer he owned and sold to Crane in 2006 (Crane then bought MEI), and six consecutive terms on the Gaming Standards Association board in Las Vegas developing the industry standards now in use. On that footing he laid out the product line: machine reporting across the route, bill dispensing, and **recycling** — cassettes that pay out from the notes already taken in, removing the refill run. He offered two things aimed squarely at Nebraska's new hand-pay problem (see the J&J segment on the same issue): splitting a payout above the $1,200 threshold into two smaller ones, and a ticket-only design where printers and bill acceptors come out of the machines entirely, replaced by a small optical QR reader, with one printed QR code carried between machines and redeemed at the recycler. Dana liked the split-payout idea. Val also described the InComm route — physical cash converted to electronic money, issued as a prepaid Mastercard and then converted again to a prize card that any ATM in the country will honour, so the player only ever sees a card dispensed. Dana's response to all of it was the same: good technology, but he doubts South Dakota would allow it.

**The QR code, which is the real operational complaint.** MG Oil's technicians are struggling with JVL's passport/QR workflow — replace a component such as a backplane board and the software has to be reloaded and a QR code scanned. Val explained where it came from: JVL built it for its own routes in Latin America, where technicians were pulling brand-new validators out of JVL machines, selling them or moving them to their own routes, and dropping in junk. Acceptance rates fell, players walked to the next machine, and revenue dropped. So each machine gets a passport at manufacture recording every serial number, sanctioned spares are pre-scanned into inventory, and an unsanctioned part simply does not get a code. The second reason was settings discipline — one canonical configuration per territory instead of a paper sheet each technician mis-copies, and the ability to change bet limits or denominations across the whole route at once. Val offered to remove the whole mechanism free of charge in the next release. What Dana actually wants is narrower and more useful: **keep** the QR scan for the initial machine setup, because MG Oil does not want technicians changing settings either, and remove only the reload-on-component-change. Better still, give him **portal access** so a technician can pull the code himself instead of photographing the screen, phoning it in, and waiting at the location with a player standing by — *"I can hurry up, get done, get out of the location, get the player back."* Parts arrived fast; the code wait was the only slow step. Val's read was that portal access is probably easier for JVL than ripping the feature out, and he will confirm with his engineers. Dana will canvass his team and come back with what to keep. Val also mentioned Keno games in the next release.

**The office.** Val asked directly whether MG Oil needs JVL to keep a South Dakota office, since they are effectively its only reason to exist. Dana's answer was no — *"I don't think it would be a problem not having somebody in South Dakota. Nebraska is not that far away."* What he needs is spare parts on hand and a person on the phone who knows the machine. Val proposed keeping a stock of parts and spare machines in a caged area of MG Oil's own warehouse, with a technician travelling in monthly to do repairs and swaps, and moving the office to Nebraska where there are more customers. The lease with Troy is month-to-month; Dana will raise the move with Troy, who is back in town the next day.

**Where the account stands.** The last of ten machines were going in that week. Troy wants **60–90 days of numbers** before deciding on further purchases. MG Oil's accounting is behind because of a **ransomware attack in January** that encrypted everything on their servers — no usable backup, a California recovery firm has clawed back much of the data at length and expense, and Dana had to rebuild from scratch. (Val compared notes: JVL was hit two years ago, caught the intrusion on an alarm, shut down, restored from backup in two days, and has since moved fully to the cloud.) Val asked to meet Troy; Dana suggested emailing him directly and offered Sioux Falls as an alternative. Dana also flagged that Whitey and Mike will want to talk to Val about Nebraska and about the **pay limit change starting in December**, and offered to make the introduction — Val asked him to open the thread and let Val take it from there, keeping Dana copied.

## Decisions Made

- **JVL will move its regional office from South Dakota to Nebraska** — Dana confirmed no local presence is needed beyond spare parts and phone support; Nebraska has more customers. Lease with Troy is month-to-month.
- **Parts stock to be held in MG Oil's own warehouse cage**, with a JVL technician travelling in monthly for repairs and swaps, instead of a dedicated South Dakota office and person.
- **Portal access, not removal, is the likely fix for the QR code friction** — MG Oil wants the initial-setup scan kept (they don't want technicians changing settings either); only the reload-on-component-change is unwanted.
- **Dana will introduce Val to Whitey Tolliver and Mike Kotab** for the Nebraska business and the December pay-limit change; Val takes the conversation over and keeps Dana copied.
- **No further machine purchases until Troy sees 60–90 days of numbers** on the ten units just installed.
