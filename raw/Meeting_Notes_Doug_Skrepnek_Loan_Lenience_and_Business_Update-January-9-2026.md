## Summary

A New Year lender call. The purpose surfaces at the end: Val asks Doug for lenience on the remaining debt repayment schedule, and spends the body of the call showing him why the business justifies patience. This is the earliest captured episode of the Skrepnek lending relationship that resurfaces in August 2026 (funding crisis and CRA-refund bridge notes).

**Val's framing:** "In terms of cash, we're in a bad position. But in terms of business, we probably have never been stronger." Georgia sales levelled off exactly as predicted, competition arrived as expected, and JVL found new markets.

**South Dakota.** A ~40-year-old, very mature, very small market (~9,000 machines) that the big manufacturers ignore. Equipment is antiquated and unsupported, so it is being replaced. One operator historically owns ~70% of the market; JVL was properly introduced, built the relationship, and survived an approval process that ran eight months longer than usual (licensing plus lottery-corporation internal approvals). The operator tested the product for 90 days in four locations, then placed a standing order: about 10 machines a month at $13,000 per machine (~$120K/month annualised across the year — below retail, but Val is "content" given the buyer controls 70% of the market). Val's logic on stickiness: the more he buys, the more locked-in he is, since there is no secondary market in the state. Val's steady-state view: ~20 machines a month for years — the total market is ~450 machines/year and operators want to dilute dependence on the dominant competitor, so ~50% share is plausible even without being number one.

**Nebraska.** Small but growing at 40% a year (~4,000 machines now). JVL is in with the big South Dakota operator (expanding into Nebraska) plus the two largest Nebraska operators. Approved in November, but per-machine licences renew annually in January, so testing waited for the new licence year: the first nine machines are placing now, a shorter 30–60-day test, with Val expecting ~10 machines a month. A relocated JVL hire spent ~8 months grooming the market — Val's description: a service-oriented "simpleton" who fishes, hunts and speaks the local language, a deliberate cultural fit.

**Commercial discipline.** JVL takes no participation deals — "we want our money relatively quickly": longest credit is six months to select operators paying monthly; non-payers' machines stop working, so bad debt is ~0.x%, practically nil.

**Georgia.** Down from 40–50 machines a month to ~20, with price sliding toward $10,000 to hold that volume. Hardware cost ~$3,500 ($4,000 all-in with duty), excluding soft costs — "engineering is a bugger."

**The transactional bet (where the cash went).** Last year's heavy engineering spend built an entirely new non-gaming product line: a POS, a redemption kiosk, and peripheral devices inside the machine. The driver is Georgia's legislation: no gambling, no cash payout — skill machines redeem credits for products, which spawned a black market of locations illegally buying credits. From June 1 the new law requires all won credits to go onto a MasterCard prepaid card; JVL partnered with InComm (the $6B last-mile prepaid provider) and wedges its POS between machine and printer to issue the card. Phase two is the redemption kiosk: a second, player-transparent cycle converts to a prize card cashable at an ATM for a $2 service fee — JVL and InComm split it $1/$1. Phase three thinking: replace the expensive third-party ATM (a $15–20K fortified mini-bank needing cash refills, float, insurance) with a JVL side-mount bill-to-bill recycler — money played in is stored in denomination cassettes and paid back out, no refill infrastructure needed at a gas station. Every cycle carries recurring revenue. Val stressed, repeatedly, that NONE of this is factored into the cash flow or P&L he has shown Doug — the numbers only carry the drain of building it. InComm is "literally begging" JVL to go further into this business, opening more location services. Val's rationale for the pivot: symbiotic with existing clients and locations, a business he knows inside out from CashCode (sold 2006), recurring revenue, and transactional businesses trade at steady, higher multiples — Doug agreed enthusiastically.

**Texas.** Skill gaming is being legalised; the kiosk demand there is the biggest — "Texas is not a state, that's a country." Val met a potential Texas hire the day before. Only a tiny cash-flow contribution is expected from July.

**The ask.** All the good news comes with no cash: "when it comes to paying the remaining of the debt that we owe you, I'm asking for your lenience." Val suggested the P&L may turn around August–September and hard times may be over by autumn. February payment is not workable; March yes. They agreed to schedule for March, and Doug (per the close) was supportive; a dinner was floated. Doug separately mentioned trying to recover $50,000 he had told Cindy about (his side, garbled) and that he is doing some business in Texas himself.
