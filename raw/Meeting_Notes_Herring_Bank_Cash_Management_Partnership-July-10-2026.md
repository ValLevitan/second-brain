## Summary

First call between Val and Maya Fuentes of Herring Bank to explore a cash-management partnership for JVL's Georgia gaming business. Maya has spent her whole career in the payments space (25+ years) and is based in Morristown, New Jersey.

Val walked through JVL's background: a 43-year-old, third-generation family business that started as a coin-op machine repair shop, became a prominent amusement-machine maker (grew to 50% market share), then entered transactional equipment in 1990 as a shareholder in a company that became a global leader in bill acceptors. That company was sold in 2006. Val later converted the amusement business into a gaming-equipment maker, which led to Georgia.

Georgia changed its law to allow transactional equipment, so JVL is re-entering that space. JVL is buying back the recycler technology it originally invented (from the company it sold to) and building a slim version — currently called the "slim recycler," likely to be marketed as a "slim ATM."

Val explained the envisioned use case, which he said Maya is only the second person he's opened up to about (the first being "John"):
- The slim recycler is mounted between gaming machines.
- A player inserts $20 and gets a QR code on paper, used as a temporary e-wallet to move between machines.
- When done, the player returns to the recycler, presents the QR code, and the machine runs three back-to-back transactions in the back office: (1) load winnings to a prepaid Mastercard, (2) buy a "prize card" service, (3) redeem the prize card for cash at JVL's own ATM function. The player only sees the QR code go in and cash come out.
- Cash is dispensed from money already stored in the recycler, so there's no cost to borrow, load, or transport cash for payouts.

On the banking side: the recycler has a locked "drop cassette" (multiple metal containers with handles) where excess cash goes once the recycling cassettes are full. That drop cash needs periodic pickup and deposit. Val noted it would be hard for JVL to deposit cash into banks itself — this is where Herring Bank comes in.

Maya described her service: Herring would manage the armored-car / cash-in-transit (CIT) back end for pickups, which would then deposit into JVL's account at Herring Bank. When Val asked about other banks, Maya said the cash would route from the armored car through CIT to the Fed. (Transcript ends here.)

JVL's model is hardware + software (all in-house), but operators can also buy and run the product themselves; in some cases JVL would operate it directly, in which case JVL would need a CIT/armored-car service for the drop cassettes.
