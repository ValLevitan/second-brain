## Summary

The first in-person meeting with Halcyon, a week after their first order. Long, wide-ranging, and useful. It closed with an agreed first installation and a confirmed Texas site visit.

**The SkillPay announcement — the reason the mood was tense.** InComm launched the SkillPay card and emailed Georgia retailers directly that it was approved and cards were shipping with tracking numbers. Then the Georgia Lottery pushed back in public. Yash's read: the lottery is wrong to have made it public, and InComm was wrong to force the issue. He had already told his own retailers they were good to go, and most had tracking numbers, when the reversal landed. He cited **OCGA 57-21-71.1(b)**: a gift card cannot be exchanged for cash, coin or currency **by a COAM location** — and pointed out the words "by a COAM location" are the loophole. His prediction is that operators will open a non-COAM suite next door and run a gift-card buying business from there, exactly as Ohio's internet-cafés did around 2010 with gold coins.

Val's own grievance came out here: **two years ago InComm came to his office and asked him to manufacture a retail kiosk.** They shook hands, Val built it and showed it at the show — and then InComm called and told him to hold off for two years, with the threat that they would take the card away from him. He was, in his own word, pissed off.

**Val's position, stated plainly:** he wants gift card, ATM and cash personally, but the company view is longevity of the business. He wants to keep selling tomorrow, so he will not take a side that risks a bust. His stated worry is **not** which way the market goes but that **the market splits** — a split is more dangerous than everyone moving in one direction, either direction. He and Yash agreed on the remedy: the conflict should have stayed in the back room, no public statements, stop asking the regulator for clarity, stop digging. Val expects the Attorney General to get involved eventually, but nothing before the November election.

Val also relayed that he had asked **Sam Westgate** about it, and Sam told him flatly that it is illegal in Georgia — the view of a national company that does not want to get busted. That is why Val keeps the recycler out of Georgia and sells only the payment kiosk and payment solution there.

**GAMOA politics.** Yash is on the board and was careful to say this is not a grudge, but his criticism was direct: the association wants every retailer, master licensee, operator and manufacturer to be a member, and non-members get ostracised. He estimated the split on SkillPay at about **70/30 in favour**, not 50/50 — most operators he knows who are not members want SkillPay, and so do all his retailers. His view is that the minority should be absorbed into the majority, because indecision is the worst decision.

**Class A — Val asking, Yash answering.** Val said operators he has dinner with are openly interested in Class A and JVL is thinking about bringing it to market. Yash's view: Class A is good, steady, no reporting, a good cash business, and less competitive. Cash payouts are allowed up to **$50 per spin**, with no cap on how many spins. He suggested linking it to the InComm card; Val pushed back, saying InComm does not do their show.

The commercial detail Yash gave is the most valuable part: **Primero's Class A deal.** Two structures — 25/75 if Primero finances the equipment, **20/80 if the operator pays up front**. Hardware is about **$2,500–3,500 per device**, and the 25% comes off the top before the operator splits with the location. Yash committed to 25–30 units but never took delivery, for one reason: **Primero has only one game suite**, and Georgia operators demand variety. That is exactly why Val is being asked to build it. Yash's other numbers: operators tell Val Class A averages about **$40/day** (range $4 to $100), and he knows a man who filled a game room with 40–50 financed units and does not care what they earn because his capital outlay was nil.

**The warning on Class A.** Last session a bill to ban these games was sponsored by **Alan Powell** and came close to passing. Yash expects a hard push next session and told Val plainly: **think twice before spending a lot of money on it.** GAMOA is 100% against Class A. Val named the fear the Class B operators should have: Class B gets taken away and Class A is already banned, leaving them with pinball, pool tables and loot boxes. Yash said their actual stated fear is that Class A cuts Class B revenue, which funds the state scholarships.

**Texas — the real numbers.** Yash's route runs **San Antonio to Beaumont** (through Houston, to the Louisiana line), about **650 machines**, mostly gas stations, averaging about **$180 per machine per day**. *This is lower than the $250/device/day he gave on the Sep 12 call — worth reconciling.* Placement rule: two to three games per location, usually three; a new location gets six to eight months, an established one 90 to 180 days; if the hold percentage drops without incremental revenue, the cabinet comes out. He is not machine-limited but inventory-limited — he says he could place **300 cabinets in two weeks** and gets location calls every day. Enforcement is county by county; he refuses locations in bad counties. The Ken Paxton opinion letter rattled people but Yash dismisses it — an opinion, not law.

**Yash's strategic advice to Val, given twice:** do not try to sell cabinets into Texas right now. The market is full of dead Banilla inventory being shifted down from Georgia, and operators will not spend another $2,500 on a cabinet. **Put JVL's software on a JAMMA-compatible pin board** at the same price and they will buy the software and drop it into the cabinets they already own. Cabinets can follow later. What Texas wants in a cabinet is big screen, bezel to bezel, LED lights — a 43" vertical single strip like Banilla's, which is close to what JVL already has. Games that do not work there: Bravery (Yildar Studios, a Georgia manufacturer pushing into grey markets), Romero, Arreiro — Yash bought them and they sit in his warehouse.

He also named the competitive model to beat: **Fusion Game Group**, placing games at about **$10 per device per day** with no split, no commitment, return them any time. That lets capital-less operators undercut incumbents on the location split.

**Val's pitch — the transactional bundle.** Val laid out the offer: **buy one machine and the entire location's transactional equipment comes free** — point of sale, kiosks, all the screens, and the AFT — with a recurring **$90 per month per location**. He then explained what the AFT actually buys the operator, and this is the part that landed:

- Promo credits issued on cash-out in exchange for a scanned barcode build the **operator's own customer database** — moving the customer away from "my location, my customers" to the operator. A location that drops you can then be answered by texting those players to the store next door.
- Under SAS rules promo credits sit in the promo meter and **cannot be cashed out**, only played — so the operator gives away nothing real.
- The eWallet moves a player machine to machine, with **partial redemption** — transfer $5, $10, $25 or all of it, chosen on the AFT display. Val called partial redemption the killer application for Georgia: with TITO, the moment a player wins big they walk, because feeding $300 into one machine is unacceptable. Partial deposit pulls them back in $10 at a time.

**Where it landed.** Yash's honest objection: his existing locations are already fitted with **Axcess** equipment. His counter-offer is to use JVL in **new** locations, and to try a couple in his **high-volume** locations for the data. Val agreed. **First installation: one JVL machine plus the transactional equipment, first week of November** — Yash offered October and Val said he would not be ready.

**Texas site visit confirmed: Friday, October 2.** Yash asked for the 2nd (he is training a new man and a colleague from India on the 1st). Plan: Val flies into San Antonio, possibly private, no pickup — he will take an Uber. Yash will show the **warehouse, Hackberry, Oranthis** (all walkable, a triangle) and then **Nix**, one of his best locations with eight or nine games, then lunch, then Val leaves. **About four hours.** Yash texts the address.

**Industry background Val collected.** Yash gave a long account of Georgia's operator history — how United Gaming's founders got started as technicians for a big pre-lottery operator; that United and Epic are both in trouble; that Epic's Josh and Kenny are in a dispute over a licence and JK Coin Supply, with Allstar Gaming behind it; and his diagnosis that the trouble across the board is **creative financing** — money borrowed on COVID-era numbers against locations that carry no loyalty. Val said his private-fund contacts tell him the same about United and that "the end is coming". Val also told the story of how JVL entered Georgia: Lucky Bucks (Seven Aces Holdings) asked for equipment, he flew down, built it in three months, and they bought by the container.
