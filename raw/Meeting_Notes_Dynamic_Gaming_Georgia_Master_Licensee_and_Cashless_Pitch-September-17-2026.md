## Summary

A long, blunt sales meeting with a Georgia master licensee who runs roughly 130 to 150 locations. He came to JVL's office; Val changed his flight to meet him. Most of the meeting was him explaining what is wrong with the market and what a working product has to do, and Val pitching the machine-plus-cashless bundle.

**His problem with Aristocrat.** He is being forced to buy. Every location demands Aristocrat One, Two, Three; if he does not supply them he loses the location — "they threaten me". He has just bought 65 machines. Aristocrat is raising prices and giving a discount only right now, three-month terms, which he called buying at gunpoint. He expected the vertical Aristocrat Two to fail against dual-screen and it did not; Three is "killing it". Val's read: Aristocrat holds about 65% of the Australian market, which is recurring revenue, so its cash flow lets it behave this way — and that IGT once had that same position and destroyed it by selling its own revenue stream to hit public-company targets.

**His problem with IGT.** He found the Prize Right payout bug himself, lost $150,000 across five locations, was told by IGT he was the only one complaining, so he went to the master community and five other masters confirmed it. IGT pulled the game from the field, fixed it, re-released — and he now has eight versions of Prize Right running. Of his **127 Prize Right machines, only 37 make money**, averaging **$238/day**; the other 90 are break-even or negative, even in Class A locations. Aristocrat, by comparison, makes about **$400**. He also has 60 Fort Knox machines he plans to pull.

**How the competing redemption systems are priced.** He gave real numbers. Novomatic's product: no charge for harness or membership, behind-the-counter device $3,500-$3,600, kiosk $7,500. The system he actually uses (Fiery Pay / "Afshar's") is $600 per machine plus $250 per connection, connects to all six machines in a location, redeems a gift card at 35 cents regardless of dollar amount, charged to the store. He is on it because it is finished and works — "it's a bandage for right now for the store owners that are bothering me too much", not a permanent solution. He tried Novomatic and it "failed miserably". He said he told friends he was going to Fiery Pay and they all followed; they took 9-11% market share quickly, 50-60 applications a day.

**His design requirements for JVL.** Stated plainly and repeatedly:
- **Behind-the-counter redemption, not self-service.** The location owner wants control and the social contact with the player. Players at a gas station are waiting for a sandwich — they will not press many buttons or create their own card. They will do all that in a casino, not here.
- **One printer behind the register**, not six or nine printers to refill. Bill acceptor stays inside the machine.
- **Split transaction** — a $500 win split across cash back to the machine, lottery, and "other gift card". A generic **"other"** button is enough; the store decides which card it hands over. TITO vouchers cannot do this; he considers JVL's approach better here.
- **Replay** — already present.
- Loose cards behind the register, not a dispenser.

**The unclaimed-funds hole.** The sharpest technical moment. He walked Val through Georgia law: money on a carrying medium that is never redeemed becomes unclaimed funds, reportable on the store owner's tax return, mandatory after seven years, with no dormancy fee and no service fee allowed. The split is 43.5% to the master, 13% to the Georgia Lottery, 43.5% retained — or keep 100% and pay tax on it. JVL's design charges $5/month on unused funds from month 13 and had configurable 30/60/90-day expiry. **Val called it outright — "it's a hole in our design"** — and the team landed on holding e-wallet balances for seven years, which removes the need for any expiry configuration at all.

**The commercial offer.** Val's package: the machine at **$15,750**, and free with it — six FlexPorts (one installed plus five more) and one kiosk per location, which he valued at about $15,000. "Basically your cost is $750." Plus a $90/month connection fee per location. The kiosk contains bill acceptor, printer, card dispenser and scanner; it dispenses a card, not cash. Val framed the whole thing as **"we're buying the market"** and would not offer him a better price than anyone else, because the offer is standardised post-show.

**The fee argument, and how it settled.** He refused flatly to carry the $90/month himself — "I cannot be paying for 130, 150 locations, $90 a month". Val warned him this puts him at a marketing disadvantage because other operators will pay it; he answered that he has seven-year contracts. They settled on JVL billing the location directly by voided check and pre-authorised debit, exactly the way Ugo/InComm and Fiery Pay already do it — and if a location stops paying, JVL turns the service off and turns it back on when it clears. Val agreed but said he would call first rather than hit the switch, and that JVL has never actually used a kill switch.

**Confidentiality.** He asked twice about protecting his location data. Val offered to sign an NDA, to destroy the data on request, and said he would never let JVL hold records that could be sold to a casino or a competing master. No contract or lock-in was required for the equipment.

**Texas.** He has around 20 to 40 JVL dual-screen cabinets sitting in a warehouse that locations displaced with Aristocrats. Val's suggestion: move them to Texas. He was quick on it — two machines per gas station alongside the incumbent Banilla vendor, ten locations to start, expand only if revenue follows. JVL's conversion software is $2,500/machine, the recycler roughly $12,000-$15,000, the port about $1,000. He asked for a package price for 30-40 conversions plus 10 recyclers, mostly San Antonio; Val said he would work it with Felipe and Andrei and come back.

**Does adding machines actually grow a location?** Val challenged him with a named-but-unnamed operator's claim, backed by nine million data points a day, that going from one Aristocrat to two to three never increases a location's long-term revenue. He answered with his own case: a location doing $2,500-$3,000 a week on Banillas, re-equipped with five IGTs, negative for three months and then $30,000 a week for six years. Val's counter was that this is competitive displacement and RTP games, not genuine growth — once the neighbouring location matches the equipment, the gain disappears. He conceded he sees the increase only "in a few locations".

**The debrief (last 20 minutes, JVL only).** Val's read was that two things landed: the giveaway, and the Texas conversion. He watched the visitor phone another operator mid-meeting to ask whether they knew JVL was giving equipment away. Felipe's criticism was that the giveaway was played too late and the meeting had stalled before it; Val accepted the point. Andrei argued the machine itself does not impress and the redemption solution is what sells, and worried the giveaway devalues the machine. Val's answer: "In America there is no better price than free" — and the honest framing is that JVL is buying the market. They agreed to rephrase the pitch as a limited-time post-show package at $15,750, while keeping "free" as the punchline.

**Two admissions in the debrief worth recording.** Andrei said he had understated JVL's priorities — **split redemption is not possible in MVP1** — but that the order can be rearranged. And he said this master is far from the first to ask for split: at the show "every second one" asked whether the redemption amount could be split into lottery tickets. Val's longer worry is that the InComm card kills the split use case entirely, and that a six-billion-dollar company will lobby it through; he does not understand InComm's free cashback and wants it tested in live locations because "something fishy" is not being disclosed.

**Loose end — receivables.** The visitor asked for relief on an outstanding balance (repair parts, just over $10,000, some past 260 days). Val refused to discount a year-old account receivable — he will not declare bad debt to his investors — but offered to give breaks on new business instead.

## Decisions

- E-wallet and unclaimed balances will be held for seven years. No expiry configuration, no dormancy charge, no service fee — Georgia law makes the store owner liable to report unclaimed funds.
- Redemption goes behind the counter by default, with self-service as an option, not the other way round. The player may start at a kiosk but redemption can be forced to the counter.
- Split redemption needs only three destinations: cash back to a machine, lottery, and a generic "other gift card" button. JVL does not need to know which card the store hands over.
- One printer behind the register per location; the bill acceptor stays inside the machine.
- JVL bills the location directly ($90/month, voided check and pre-authorised debit), not the master. Non-payment means the service is switched off, but Val will call before using it.
- No equipment contract and no lock-in period.
- Pricing is standardised post-show — no better deal for this master than for anyone else. Val will only help move his surplus machines to Texas.
- Val will not discount a year-old accounts receivable; relief comes on new business instead.
- The pitch is reworked to put the giveaway up front, framed as a limited-time post-show package.
