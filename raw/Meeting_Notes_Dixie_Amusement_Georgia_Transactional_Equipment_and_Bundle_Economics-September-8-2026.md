## Summary

**Val put a hard offer on the table for the first time.** Until now Dixie has been testing two JVL machines and deliberately staying out of transactional equipment. This time Val made the pitch directly: *"Did you align yourself with anybody in transactional equipment here? Why not align yourself with me?"* The offer, as stated in the room:

- Buy **one** new vertical-screen machine with fifteen games (the machine Salim's own figures put at **$294/day**; Val also said "$390 something" once — the two numbers do not agree and neither was reconciled).
- Get **five additional FlexPorts free**, plus a counter-top/behind-the-counter unit **free** — optical reader, bill acceptor and ticket printer, "no gimmicks".
- **$90 per location per month**, all in. That includes the on-site mini server, the cloud backup server, and TITO — all JVL's own, no third party.
- **Operator intelligence** is free for now and will eventually become a paid option: collection and service reporting, popularity reports, "machine one has not played for three days, send a technician", cash-door-open alerts, and bill-acceptor first-insertion acceptance rate falling below 65% ("time to clean your bill acceptor") — pushed to the operator's phone.

Salim's reaction to the price was *"it's unbearable"* — meaning unbeatable — and he said "okay", then immediately pulled back from shaking hands: *"We are on the upper casing. I don't want to shake hands and then back out."* His benchmark: the cheapest competitor he has seen is **$13/month/machine**, the most expensive **$1/day/machine**.

**Then Salim turned it around and taught Val the card economics.** This is the substantive new intelligence from the meeting. He has been running InComm gift cards and Fiery Pay side by side for four months and compares data with a group of master licensees.

- **Average load per card: ~$280.** The broker earns **$1** per card issued.
- InComm told Val the market averages **1.5 cards/day/location**. Salim's own current number is **3.5–4 cards/day**. He accepts InComm's figure was probably true *before* July 1 — pre-July-1 was the pilot, nobody was in a hurry, and InComm was buying market share (he believes they gave equipment away). After July 1 the volume is real.
- **The broker's risk is the whole problem.** Ten cards at $280 is $2,800 the broker has fronted; if the location's ACH does not clear, the broker is out $2,800 and holding $6 of revenue. Val asked whether he could shut the terminal down to enforce payment. Salim said as a master licensee he would not accept that — it is not his fault, and the Lottery would treat the shutdown as more evidence the device sits in the regulatory grey area.
- **Salim says InComm absorbs that risk for brokers.** Val had never heard this — he asked InComm two years ago and was told no. He committed to going back to them.
- **Why the master doesn't just become the broker and keep the $1:** InComm has offered exactly that to Salim's group and they refuse it. On 70 stores at 3 cards/day it is about $100/month, and one service call for a jammed or empty card dispenser eats it. He also does not want his locations knowing he earns anything there, because it weakens him when he negotiates with them. Val's counter — JVL processes it automatically, reports to his phone, InComm carries the risk — did not move him, though he did not close the door.
- **Fiery Pay is backed by Fiserv** as program manager (Salim spelled it out); InComm is bigger. InComm is a *program manager*, not the provider — the provider is a bank entity. Salim's repeated question to Val was *"do you have a bank?"* — his view is that owning the rails, not the $1, is where the money is.

**Georgia regulatory and political read (Salim speaking as GAMOA VP).** The association is glad the transactional equipment is coming but wants it slow-walked and timed. Gift cards became legal in the market on **July 1, 2026**. You cannot go to a legislator weeks later and ask that a gift card be cashable at an ATM. Tax history: started at 5%, crept up year by year to 10%, then jumped to **30%** in 2024 when the industry asked for the gift card, now sits at **13%**. Ask for something else and it jumps again. Statutory constraint: a location may not derive more than **50% of store gross revenue** from COAM. Georgia regulates three tiers only — distributor (JVL), master licensee, location.

**Who pays the monthly fee.** Masters are currently pushing the connection fee onto locations, which Val found extraordinary — in his experience (operator in Canada from 1984, then large fleets in Mexico, Peru and Colombia) the location never pays for anything and the split never beats 60/40 in the master's favour. Both agreed the push onto locations is a negotiating shield and that the master will end up paying, because the master buys the equipment.

**Class A.** Salim's position: Class A is barely regulated — roughly a **$25 sticker**, no tax, no regulation — while Class B pays 13% plus licensing plus cost of doing business. Class A exists for jukeboxes, pool tables and skill-ball; somebody has built a machine that plays like Class B and filed it as Class A. He does not think it survives: Class B will push the Lottery to tax Class A at 13% too, and the moment that happens every Class B operator jumps across — but the genuine Class A world (pinball, Chuck E. Cheese, Dave & Buster's) has the bigger lobby and will not pay 13%. Expect infighting, and a short-term grey-area window that closes.

**Single-SAS.** Val disclosed that he and Andrei had spent two hours the same day on it and concluded the single-SAS market disappears. JVL has dual-SAS and single-SAS capability today; Salim confirmed the Lottery will not allow check-in on single SAS. Val's sizing: ~**10,000** single-SAS machines installed of ~**47,000** in the market; ~10% replacement a year ≈ **4,000 machines**, of which 70–80% displace single-SAS ≈ **3,300/year**, so three to five years and it is gone. Revenue per single-SAS machine he put at $60–70/month, probably closer to $40, against a state average of $108 per machine. Conclusion Val floated: stop supporting single SAS altogether and connect to the second SAS port only. Salim put dual SAS at ~7% of the market today.

**Installation — asked, priced, then explicitly not promised.** Salim's own crew costs him ~$150/hour, two people, ~5 hours per location, so **~$1,000 per location**, more when the drive is long (he named routes of four hours each way). At 60–70 locations that is **~$70,000**. Val asked "if I told you that you don't have to pay seventy thousand, can we make the deal today?" — and then, when Salim started treating it as an offer, pulled it back cleanly: *"I didn't say anything about installation. Let's just be clear. I didn't promise anything. I just asked the question."* **The $70,000 is not on the table.**

**Servicing is the master's obligation by law.** A location may not repair or maintain the equipment — only the master licensee — a security provision written into the law around 2014/2017. The live legal question is whether the transactional device counts as *part of* the machine: if it does, the Lottery regulates it; if it is a freestanding device, it does not. It is being argued both ways, and Salim notes the device is communicating with the machine, which is the weak point in the "freestanding" argument.

**"First Nation pricing."** Precedent from 2015: Lee Hunter, who had the IGT distribution for Georgia, asked Salim to assemble a group of Indian-American master licensees to buy 100 units, on condition nobody got a better price than the group. Salim ran it and about 12–15 masters bought; another twenty asked what Dixie was buying. Val offered the same deal now: *"Help me secure all your friends in one custom deal, and I'll give you First Nation prices"* — same price to others, never cheaper.

**Market structure numbers Salim gave:** 190 master licences but only ~80 real operators; of ~60 in his orbit, maybe 20 think for themselves and 40 are "monkey see, monkey do".

**Removing the bill acceptor.** Val floated eliminating the bill acceptor from the machine entirely: cash goes into the kiosk, the printed ticket becomes the wallet, scan it at the machine. Salim's objection is behavioural, not technical — the 30/60-second timer means players will not walk to the kiosk mid-session, and adoption needs both the location and the player educated. The JVL colleague in the room proposed A/B testing it in selected locations first rather than deciding in the abstract. Nobody disputed that it saves real money.

**Post-meeting debrief (last two minutes, Val and the JVL colleague).** The configuration discussed is "five, six, four plus IQ and kiosks". A kiosk without the card dispenser is printer, bill receiver and scanner only — but every kiosk in the deal carries the card dispenser. He buys one game machine. **A contract will definitely be required.** He is comfortable paying the monthly fee and comfortable with the service arrangement. The open question the colleague raised — what "InComm takes the risk" means in practice — is the same one Val is taking back to InComm.

**Timing.** Salim is in D.C. Thursday for a week settling his daughter, back the following week, then Vegas for a retreat, with G2E season on top. He asked for roughly two weeks, and framed the real conversation as preparation for next year: *"In preparing for next year, we can start talking."*

## Decisions Made

- The bundle offer is now formally in front of Dixie: one fifteen-game vertical machine, five FlexPorts and a counter unit free, $90 per location per month including mini server, cloud backup and TITO.
- Installation cost is **not** being absorbed by JVL. Val stated this explicitly after Salim began to treat it as an offer.
- "First Nation pricing" is offered on the same terms as the 2015 IGT precedent, conditional on Salim assembling the group.
- Operator intelligence stays free at launch, to be priced later as an option.
- No handshake. Salim declined to close and asked for about two weeks.
- Val and Andrei's internal position on single SAS — let it die, connect to the second SAS port — was stated to a customer for the first time, but not decided.

## Open questions

- **What does "InComm takes the risk" actually mean?** Neither Val nor his colleague could pin it down, and Salim's account is second-hand. Everything about JVL becoming a broker depends on the answer.
- **Does JVL want its own bank rails?** Salim asked twice. He thinks the $1 per card is not the business; owning the rails, as Fiery Pay did through Fiserv, is.
- **Is the transactional device "part of" the machine under Georgia law?** Unresolved and contested. It determines whether the Lottery regulates it and whether a terminal shutdown for non-payment is even permissible.
- **$294 or "$390 something" per day** for the vertical machine — the two figures were used for the same machine minutes apart and were not reconciled.
- **What breakage rate should be assumed?** Salim cited the Lottery's own default figure at ~0.3% and pointed out that on $6 of revenue per $2,800 fronted, half a percent is devastating. Val said he cannot attach a number to it yet.
