## Summary

A one-on-one between Val and Michael Gottlieb about how the game-art organisation should be titled, staffed and measured. Val's stated goal: align his understanding with Michael's — and he judged it "a very positive conversation."

**The "manager" title.** Val takes the manager title "extremely seriously" — a manager is an executive-type role responsible for cost-oriented elements (schedule, processes), not someone who merely manages their own work: "I cannot call everybody a manager." Michael's industry counterpoint: in the slot/game world the title is looser (an art asset manager can be a low-paid individual contributor — Bally, Microsoft, Google all do it), and in the US the title was abused to make hourly workers salaried (the McDonald's example). Val refuses that game: no manager titles as a "cute way not to pay hourly" — he wants bureaucracy and politics stripped out. What Michael actually needs under Lena is a senior person who can take a top-level directive, break it down, schedule the pieces and execute without hand-holding (his example: Anastasia, who was given top-level direction in a vacuum and produced "the blueprints of how we're going to make this game"). Michael proposed the real industry title **Art Production Lead**; Val took it under consideration — "at least right now we're in sync." Val also restated his hiring economics: seniority and qualification first, sourced where the market is favourable (Poland, Portugal, Spain, Italy, Belarus, Russia, Ukraine), salary set by supply and demand.

**Automation of Lena's administration.** Val declared intent to automate 95% of Lena's administrative (non-value-producing) work in 2026 — "not in the future, this year" — and noted the art production pipeline is "about twice as complicated as anything I've ever been exposed to," to be optimised this year. Michael added that Lena is senior yet open to AI with no resistance — unlike art directors elsewhere who feel threatened.

**How estimation works today (Michael's account).** Three inputs: (1) metrics — what the previous comparable took (Georgia Peach jackpot as the metric for the Gold Nugget jackpot), adjusted for added complexity; (2) the employee's own estimate; (3) Lena's layer — not just assessment but guidance and shortcuts ("of those seven days, what is the biggest piece? Do we even need it?"), strategising with Michael on bang-for-buck ("is the juice worth the squeeze" — the prospector needs to look good, "but it's not Pixar"). Feedback loops run within hours, not weeks; Lena reassigns tasks when someone spins their wheels. Deadlines often work backwards (pull, not push).

**Val's estimation model.** Stories should be sized so that with a known iteration norm (he tested 2.2–2.5, Michael countered that iterations are actually a small share — often right first time, so Val recalibrated to ~1.3) a story maps to predictable hours; ten tasks on a senior designer ≈ 30 hours of load, and a whole game becomes estimable in hours. Michael: "that's the way it works — to a great extent we have been doing that."

**The consistency gap (the one concrete finding).** Val asked what a story costs; Michael: one hour up to a day or two. Val: "that's a big gap — we're not breaking out stories consistently." He recommended uniform story sizing, noting he and Lena identified this a year ago but lacked bandwidth. Michael pushed back on over-granularity: too-fine tasks are "deceptive" (you hit task five on schedule while the hard part hides at the end); he manages by final shippable assets — "I need final assets, not completed tasks." Val's synthesis: different layers live in different worlds — Michael lives in macro assets and ship dates, designers/animators live in tasks and deliverables; both views are true, and reopened tasks per iteration keep the arithmetic honest.

**Generalists vs specialists.** Michael: at this size, no dedicated specialists — a dedicated animator is "an excuse," and specialist pipelines make everything regress to the same look; he wants kick-ass generalists whose strongest dish is animation (his ex-studio example: Anthony). Val defended animator/illustrator as genuinely narrow specialities that generalists do badly — then conceded the point is moot: the market barely produces good computer-native animators/illustrators anyway ("if you find a really good one, they love pencil, not computer"), so JVL ends up with generalists regardless. Michael added that Aristocrat's specialists are even more miserable ("nothing but simple animation as a full-time job — awful"). Val closed the thread with the wildcard: Adobe is about to push heavy automation this year, and 2027 "should be a killer year" — a new generation with different tools will change task durations, which is exactly why he is pressing on duration-per-task now.

## Decisions Made

- **No inflated "manager" titles at JVL** — the role under Lena is a senior self-directed executor; "Art Production Lead" is the candidate title, Val to think on it. (Val Levitan, Michael Gottlieb)
- **Story sizing must be made consistent** — the current one-hour-to-two-days spread breaks estimation. (Val Levitan; Michael acknowledged with the granularity caveat)
- **Working iteration norm ~1.3 per task** for load arithmetic, superseding Val's 2.2–2.5 opening assumption. (recalibrated live)
- **Hiring reality accepted: generalists with a strong animation/illustration speciality**, since true specialists are unavailable in the market. (converged)
