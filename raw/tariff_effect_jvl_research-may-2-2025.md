# Dodging the tariff bullet: How slot machine maker JVL can navigate US-China-Canada trade tensions

## The bottom line

JVL faces a perfect storm of tariffs in its current supply chain, with Chinese components potentially subject to 145% tariffs and Canadian-made products hit with a 25% US import tariff since March 2025. To survive, JVL must immediately optimize its USMCA documentation and potentially redesign its manufacturing process to ensure products qualify as "Canadian-origin" under the substantial transformation test. The most effective mitigation strategies include operating within US Foreign Trade Zones, implementing duty drawback programs, and strategically diversifying component sourcing away from China. Gaming manufacturers who've already implemented these approaches have reduced effective tariff rates from 25% to under 8%.

## Current tariff reality for JVL's cross-border business

JVL operates within a complex web of tariffs affecting every part of its supply chain. The company's slot machines (classified under HS code 9504.30) face these current rates:

- **Components from China to Canada**: 0-6.5% Canadian import tariffs on electronic components under Most-Favored Nation (MFN) rates
- **Components from South Korea to Canada**: Mostly duty-free under the Canada-Korea Free Trade Agreement
- **Finished machines from Canada to US**: 0% if USMCA-compliant; **25% if non-compliant** (since March 2025)

For JVL's products to qualify as USMCA-compliant and avoid the 25% US tariff, they must meet either:
1. A **tariff shift requirement** (non-originating materials undergo sufficient transformation to change classification)
2. A **regional value content (RVC) threshold** of 60% using the Transaction Value Method or 50% using the Net Cost Method

The critical factor is whether JVL's manufacturing process qualifies as a "substantial transformation" of the Chinese and South Korean components. US Customs evaluates this based on manufacturing complexity, value added in Canada, and whether the finished slot machine has a different "name, character, or use" than its imported components.

## Recent tariff policy changes reshaping JVL's situation

The tariff landscape has deteriorated significantly in 2025:

- **February 2025**: US imposed 10% baseline tariff on all Chinese imports
- **March 2025**: Increased to 20% on Chinese imports; **25% tariff added on Canadian goods** entering the US
- **April 2025**: Multiple increases culminated in a **145% total tariff on Chinese goods**

These changes create multiple tariff exposure points for JVL:
1. Chinese components face up to 145% tariffs if considered to "pass through" Canada without substantial transformation
2. Even products manufactured in Canada now face 25% US import tariffs unless they qualify under USMCA

US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) has also ramped up enforcement with a **162% increase in anti-circumvention determinations** compared to previous administrations, specifically targeting operations in countries like Canada that might be used to avoid Chinese tariffs.

## How US-China tensions affect Canadian manufacturing

JVL's position—manufacturing in Canada with Chinese components for US export—is precisely the type of supply chain most affected by current trade tensions. Key issues include:

1. **Dual tariff risk**: Products could potentially face both Chinese component tariffs and Canadian manufacturing tariffs unless they qualify for USMCA preferential treatment.

2. **Anti-circumvention scrutiny**: US authorities are actively investigating companies that appear to be doing minimal processing in third countries to avoid Chinese tariffs. Red flags include:
   - Simple assembly operations with limited value addition
   - Recent shifts in manufacturing locations following tariff implementation
   - Minimal transformation of Chinese components

3. **Documentation burden**: JVL must maintain extensive records showing:
   - The origin of all components and materials
   - Manufacturing processes performed in Canada
   - Supply chain documentation for Chinese components
   - Calculations demonstrating Regional Value Content

Companies implementing similar supply chains have been forced to dramatically reduce their dependence on Chinese components, with some electronics manufacturers cutting Chinese content from 60% to 35% by shifting to suppliers in Taiwan, South Korea, and Mexico.

## Gaming equipment tariff specifics

Gambling equipment imported to the US from Canada faces specific tariff considerations:

1. **HS Classification**: Slot machines are classified under HS 9504.30 (Games operated by coins, banknotes, bank cards, tokens or similar means of payment)

2. **Non-USMCA Rate**: 25% as of March 2025

3. **USMCA Qualification Path**: To qualify as "originating" under USMCA, JVL's slot machines must either:
   - Meet a specific tariff shift requirement, where all non-originating materials change classification
   - Achieve an RVC of 60% under the Transaction Value Method or 50% under the Net Cost Method

4. **Electronic Component Classification**: Components may fall under various HS codes depending on function (typically Chapter 85), and each has its own specific rule of origin under USMCA

Interestingly, there's a **10% de minimis provision** in USMCA (up from 7% under NAFTA), allowing up to 10% of non-originating materials that don't meet tariff shift requirements to be disregarded in origin determination.

## USMCA provisions offering potential relief

The USMCA replaced NAFTA with several changes relevant to JVL:

1. **Documentation flexibility**: USMCA eliminated the standardized Certificate of Origin (Form 434) in favor of more flexible documentation. JVL must provide nine specific data elements, including:
   - Certifier information
   - Origin criteria met
   - Description and HS classification
   - Authorized signature and date

2. **Rules of origin calculations**: The methodology changed to count the value of ALL non-originating materials, eliminating the "free-riding" under NAFTA where certain parts could count as originating even if produced overseas.

3. **Certification responsibility**: Certification can be completed by producer, exporter, or importer, giving JVL more flexibility in documentation management.

4. **Record-keeping requirements**: JVL must maintain all records related to origin for five years, including component sourcing documentation, manufacturing records, RVC calculations, and shipping records.

5. **Verification process**: JVL should expect potential verification by US Customs through written requests, questionnaires, or even visits to manufacturing facilities.

## Practical strategies to minimize tariff impact

Successful gaming and electronics manufacturers have implemented these proven strategies to mitigate tariff impacts:

### Supply chain restructuring
- **Diversify component sourcing** with a "China+Many" approach, exploring suppliers in Taiwan, Malaysia, and Vietnam
- **Create supplier redundancy** by qualifying multiple sources for critical components
- **Establish dual-country manufacturing** with core production in Canada and final assembly in the US, potentially in a Foreign Trade Zone

### Customs programs with immediate benefits
- **Utilize Foreign Trade Zones (FTZs)** to defer, reduce, or eliminate duties on components
- **Implement duty drawback** to reclaim up to 99% of duties paid on imported components when finished products are exported
- **Obtain binding rulings** from US Customs for favorable product classifications

### USMCA optimization tactics
- **Document transformation processes** to demonstrate "substantial transformation" of Chinese components
- **Strategically restructure manufacturing** to meet tariff shift or regional value content requirements
- **Implement robust certification systems** to maximize duty-free treatment for qualifying products

### Tariff engineering approaches
- **Analyze and modify products** to qualify for more favorable tariff classifications
- **Ship certain components separately** from main units, creating "kits" that may receive different tariff treatment
- **Redesign products** to increase North American content and qualify for USMCA benefits

## What this means for JVL

JVL can significantly reduce tariff exposure by implementing a phased approach to these strategies:

**Immediate actions (0-6 months):**
- Optimize USMCA documentation
- Implement duty drawback program
- Request binding rulings on product classifications

**Medium-term initiatives (6-12 months):**
- Establish operations in a US Foreign Trade Zone
- Begin diversifying component sourcing
- Separate high-tariff components for shipment

**Long-term transformation (12-24 months):**
- Comprehensively restructure supply chain
- Consider establishing US assembly operations
- Redesign products for optimal tariff treatment

Companies that have successfully navigated similar challenges have reduced their effective tariff rates from 25% to under 8% while maintaining product quality and competitive pricing. For JVL, implementing these strategies is not just about cost savings—it's about business survival in an increasingly complex and restrictive trade environment.
