## Summary

The third ARC meeting, and the first one that put the transactional equipment in front of Sloan. Klimovich demonstrated it, Val sold around it, and the last third turned into a live negotiation on Class A cabinets — Val putting a specific counter-offer on the table against ARC's existing Primero deal. Sloan did not accept and did not refuse: **"Let me just think on that."**

**The demo.** Klimovich walked the wallet end to end. The wallet is created on the server, protected, and carried by the player behind a PIN; the player scans at the machine, confirms the PIN and sees the balance, moves machine to machine keeping the wallet in their own hands, and collects at a kiosk or at a cashier who scans it with Flex IQ and hands over the card. Val stressed twice that **the bill acceptor and the printer are optional**. Klimovich's framing was Lego bricks — build one universal configuration for everything, or a specific build per location: a slim kiosk with optical reader and TITO ticket only, no bill validator, no card dispenser. Wallet validity is set per location, normally 30 days.

Val added what the modular story does not cover: JVL can go **fully cashless and drop tickets altogether**, transferring to the player's phone and scanning from the phone. On integration, FlexPort is an AFT device into the second SAS port, or through an adapter where there is no second port. The next version is **fully enclosed in plastic with two mounting posts for surface mounting**, for older machines with no proper cavity — the wiring is fished through the post — and **version 2 will be Wi-Fi configurable**. Units come pre-installed in the cabinets, about five minutes to install.

Two things Val pushed hardest. **Promo credits** — he can send promotional credit to a player, and when Sloan said he has an app about to roll out across all locations, Val confirmed promo credits can go into the app. And **accounting with no manpower**: a daily file of all of ARC's accounting, sweeping their customers' ACH into their bank and reconciling into Microsoft 365 Business Central — one file.

**Sloan's own position.** Some of his locations are completely cashless, not by choice but by the venue's corporate policy, and they qualify under **Georgia's open-air provision** — a place open more than 35 years counts as an entertainment place. He says only one location in the state has it today, about 12 machines, and they have not taken advantage of it. His own preference is cash, and he was blunt about why: a player who gets cash in hand puts it back in the machine and chases, and taking cash out of the loop loses that revenue. He may try to convert those locations to cash later.

His stated pain point is **not the equipment but the lack of a standard** — too many different technologies, expensive and slow to adopt, rebuild and roll out, with old games still on the ground. His strategy is to run locations as a portfolio: build up, flush out what does not work, rebuild. Most locations already have some gift-card solution, down to about 20 without a full kiosk; the gift card made them compliant cheaply but has **no replayability**, complaints are coming in now and they are pulling them out. On limits, he can get $5,000–10,000 a day but they will not go above it; on his own kiosks ARC is its own broker, so ARC controls the window and carries the risk, and in most locations they collect the cash themselves anyway.

**Class A — the negotiation.** Sloan raised it himself: "What about Class A? Let's go into the work."

Val's position: he brought his game designer, they looked at the location, they saw Primero, and they understand the technology because they know limited-payout machines from Germany. His own machine is too expensive and does not fit the bulk market, so it needs a real redesign — maths and graphics are not a problem, the hardware is — and **he can have it redesigned by New Year**. He was explicit that this is not on ARC's account: he needs it for other customers too.

Sloan called it a huge opportunity and said ARC will "fight like hell" on the legislation to keep it. His commercial reason is licensing speed: **Class B is harder to license for many of his locations, some taking six months, where a Class A machine can be dropped in two days to two weeks.** His placement rate is about a location a day, four or five machines a day.

The numbers, as Sloan described his **current Primero deal**: $1,000 up front plus **10%** of revenue when he uses his own cabinet, or $1,000 plus **20%** when he uses Primero's. He has not used their cabinets — he has a warehouse of refurbished cabinets to work through. His own cost per game is about **$2,500**, so $3,500 all in. Val said plainly he **cannot touch that price** because Primero is subsidising heavily. Asked for a realistic daily average per machine, Sloan would not give one; Val put roughly $30 a day, which makes Primero's 10% about $3 a day at no ongoing cost to them.

**Val's counter-offer**, stated precisely: JVL takes possession of ARC's existing cabinets and scraps them, gives ARC a **brand-new complete JVL cabinet with the game**, for **$2,500 per cabinet plus 20%**. Sloan's answer was to think about it — part of his reason for considering Primero's cabinet was another location he had in mind, and he would rather give up 10% and use his own box.

On flexibility, Sloan was clear he will **pay more for a game that earns more** — a game doing $60 a day against one doing $40 is worth more to him — so there is room in the deal depending on performance. Val's closing pitch was that he will rush development, build ARC's games, and go into new markets with them: "I'm a very, very good, reliable partner. And I do things on a handshake and I stick with it." Sloan: "Send me a message."

**Relationship intelligence.** Val said he got into the Georgia market **through Lucky Bucks, through Jamie**, and is having dinner with Jamie on Monday night. Sloan said he put Jamie into business in Illinois, that Jamie is now buying locations and cafés, and that he is happy with how it is going. Val said he **invested in that** and is happy with the investment. Both were pulled into South Dakota by Jamie; Sloan noted South Dakota licensing has got harder — a liquor licence is needed in every small jurisdiction. *(This is the same Jamie Val called at 12:50 the same day — see [[Meeting_Notes_Jamie_Georgia_Class_A_Opportunity_and_Market_Outlook-September-16-2026]].)*

**Next steps.** Sloan offered to send a couple of his people over on **Friday** to see the equipment, then said he might come himself and walk through it. He leaves Saturday for G2E, then Wednesday for two weeks in Greece, back on the 13th. Val is in Texas and Nebraska and back the week of October 12. They settled on **October 15** to pick it up.

## Decisions Made

- **JVL will build a redesigned Class A machine, targeted for New Year.** Maths and graphics are straightforward; the hardware has to be redesigned to bring the cost into the bulk market. Val stated this is for the wider market, not billed to ARC. (Val Levitan)
- **Val will not compete with Primero on price.** Primero is subsidising heavily and Val said directly he cannot touch that deal. (Val Levitan)
- **Next contact set for October 15**, after Sloan's G2E trip and two weeks in Greece and Val's Texas/Nebraska travel. (Val Levitan, David Sloan)
- **ARC people will see the transactional equipment on Friday** — Sloan first offered to send staff, then said he may come himself. (David Sloan)

Put on the table and NOT accepted: Val's offer to scrap ARC's cabinets and supply new complete JVL cabinets at **$2,500 plus 20%**. Sloan said he would think about it and asked Val to send a message. Treat this as an open offer, not a deal.

## Open Questions

- Sloan would not give a real daily average per Class A machine ("can't say it's a lot"). Val worked from ~$30/day. The economics of any deal turn on this number and it is still unknown.
- Whether ARC will move off Primero at all — Sloan has a warehouse of refurbished cabinets he would rather use at 10% than pay for new ones at 20%.
- How much performance flexibility there really is. Sloan said he would pay more for a game earning $60 against $40, but no band was agreed.
- Whether the open-air cashless provision is worth pursuing as a product angle — only one location in the state uses it, with about 12 machines, and Sloan is not enthusiastic about cashless in general.
