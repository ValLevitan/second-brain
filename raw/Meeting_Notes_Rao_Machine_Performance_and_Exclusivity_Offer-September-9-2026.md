## Summary

Rao is a distributor and installer who currently sells and installs the Access transactional system in Georgia. Val brought him in to move him onto JVL exclusively. Two arguments ran in parallel: the commercial offer, and Rao's belief that JVL machines do not earn enough.

**Rao's operation.** He put 350 locations on Access in about three to three and a half months, running three to four crews at three to five installations a day. A machine takes about 30 minutes; an IGT or Aristocrat takes three to five. A six-machine location is at least three hours. He makes about $2,500 per install on reselling the equipment. He is candid that the monthly side is not a good business: masters are often not paying the monthly fee, locations never pay, and he is currently losing a little on the subscriptions he collects for Access. He said he bought $1.5 million of Access equipment for cash, has around $250,000 he needs to collect and pay, and holds roughly $500,000 of equipment.

**The card puzzle.** Val pressed a question he said he could not resolve: he understands why a player wants cash and why a location wants to pay cash, but not why a location pays cash *without* issuing a card first. His own proposal was that the location issue the card and then buy it back at a small discount — the location buys merchandise anyway and can pay for it with the card, which satisfies the player, the lottery corporation and the law. Rao's answer was that locations feel the pressure of the two to three percent cost, and that once a card is issued the money goes onto the card and is less likely to come back to the machine. Val's counter was that locations are already breaking the law and already buying cards, so he would rather have a cost attached, and that as cards go into circulation locations will start repurchasing them instead of paying cash.

**The offer.** Val bundles one machine with all the transactional equipment free: the port in the machine plus five additional ports, one IQ point-of-sale unit, and one Pay unit. The operator pays only for the machine — $15,750 for the fifteen-game software. Val put the free equipment at about $15,000 of value (roughly six to seven thousand plus eight thousand). He framed it as buying the market, and said the offer came together after the trade show following conversations with a number of people like Rao.

What Val wants in return: Rao's undivided attention to JVL for at least two years, stopping the sale of Access; JVL exclusive in the location; and a three-year contract with the location. His reason for exclusivity was blunt — if another system is present in the location, JVL will never be able to collect. Rao's read was that an operator will not sign but a location may.

**Val's advice to liquidate Access stock.** Val urged Rao to sell his roughly $500,000 of Access equipment now, at cost, to someone who does not yet know about the free offer, or to hand it back against receivables — because once the market learns JVL gives the equipment away, Access cannot sell it. He acknowledged the pitch was aggressive.

**What Rao committed to.** He said he would think about it and work it out, then, when Val asked him directly, said "I'm in." He agreed to select five locations for October, with the first installation in the first week of October, and Val booked him for locations next week. Rao warned he cannot force Access out — it will happen organically — and Val agreed. Rao also wants the installation entity kept separate from the rest of his business.

JVL's side: internal testing first, next week, before any install. A JVL colleague said they will be ready to support the five October locations, will carry Rao's team through the learning curve, and will personally attend the first couple of installations.

**Mounting problem and Val's fix.** Rao described locations where the existing cabinets are wooden or have no base plate at all, so mounting needs cutting, and some locations are awkward to cut. Val offered on the spot to supply a fully enclosed top-mount box on two mounting posts, with the cable run through one hollow post, so nothing has to be cut.

**SAS and MudaGoose.** Val said JVL is definitely not going with MudaGoose, for the collection reason above. He said no final decision had been made between dual SAS and single SAS; Rao insisted both are needed or it will not work, and Val accepted on the spot — "you just made up our decision, we're going with both." A JVL colleague asked that the October test location be one where all machines are dual source; Rao said they already are.

**The performance dispute.** Rao's position is that JVL machines underperform — he pointed to a location where a Novomatic machine ("rocket rush") took the top position, and to IGT progressives showing six to eight thousand against JVL's one thousand, which is what a player gravitates to.

Val's position, stated repeatedly: given the same location, the same settings, the same volume and credit settings and the same bet buttons, a JVL machine should always rank in the top three. If it does not, the cause is physical or configuration — wrong RTP, wrong bet or reward button, no topper, a broken chair, or the machine placed next to a humming ice maker that blows hot air. He said he does not claim to be first everywhere, but he should be within the first three every time. He asked Rao to send the *settings* of the competitor machines and photographs of their bet buttons — not the revenue report. Rao kept offering the report; Val repeated that he wants the settings. Rao agreed to send them.

Val also offered to take the best location, pull the existing dual-screen JVL machine, set a machine up himself, put it back in the same spot and let Rao test it for free.

Val explained the trade-off he wants Rao to understand: mathematics is fully random, so paying out more costs either volatility or RTP. Jackpot Rush is deliberately built for high volatility; the other games are built for time on device, which is why their progressives differ. He also rejected the idea that JVL knows less than its competitors — his senior VP of game design came from Light & Wonder, everyone attends the same shows, and there are no secrets between JVL, Light & Wonder, Aristocrat and IGT.

Rao's counter-argument was about how installations actually happen: his technicians unload from the trailer and place machines in order, with no thought to position. He said in a thousand installations he never once considered which machine went where. Val used that to make his point — if Rao had a percentage of each machine's take he would move machines, clean them and attach a numbered chair. A JVL colleague added that the settings load from a USB stick, that the range is expandable, and that JVL can tune higher or lower given more information about a location, but that configuring blind would be irresponsible.

Val's framing of the whole exercise: "My goal is not to change the location's mind. My goal is to change your mind." He asked to be taken to locations one by one.

**A real change to the offer.** Late in the meeting Val stopped and said the team had got laser-focused on packaging the deal, but a distributor like Rao does not need the package — he can install and resell the transactional equipment on its own to operators who do not want to buy a machine. He returned to it in the closing seconds, asking the room directly whether everyone agreed the deal does not have to be packaged, and saying he would consider the package deal at the schools level. This loosens the bundle that the rest of the meeting was built on.

**Market and competitor intelligence from Rao.** There are about 5,500 locations running PAX with InComm, all of which need replacing; Val's guidance was to target locations with no system rather than replace working ones. Val said InComm will happily let JVL put its own equipment in, because InComm wants to clear cards and does not want to deal with equipment. Rao named the Fiery Pay principal as Avshad Patel, said Adam is his partner, and mentioned an Aristocrat connection. He described Ready Credit Corporation as a very small company that had an exclusive arrangement with Access to sell cards, with deals across three companies. He also described SkillPay as a crypto company that issues and buys the ATM-access card, on the theory that crypto is the only legal route, and said the cards are shifting from gift card to crypto — he was explicit that this is his own assumption from what he has heard, not established fact. Rao's own view of why the market shifted is that "Absad" is the main driver rather than InComm; a JVL colleague thought InComm mattered too, by going to locations directly.

Rao also warned that the market is close to saturated and that arriving two years ago would have been much better. Val agreed the timing would have been better but said JVL could not have had it two years ago, so there is no point discussing it, and pointed at Texas as a booming market alongside Georgia. Val mentioned he has a building in Austin with a vacant unit where an office could open. Rao's 200 locations lined up for Access turn out to be masters who already had Access, not new installations.

**A regulator-side letter.** Val raised an email dated September 2 that he had read and did not follow. A JVL colleague summarised its position: the concern is that players should not get close to cash so quickly, the expectation was that players would keep the card rather than cash it, and now technology provides immediate cash and that worries the author. Val's reaction was that this reads as a lawyer inventing limitations without considering technology, that nobody controls it, and that a player will wager cash and walk away with cash regardless — with or without an ATM. Asked directly whether JVL should change its market-entry approach, Val said no. He accepted that operators could collectively decide not to promote the system through their association, and said he was fine with that because it has nothing to do with the technology.

**Other threads.** JVL is taking 65 machines in on trade from an operator; Val asked whether Rao could use them, but Rao only sells in Georgia. Val asked for a direct line to David Milani (also said as Vilari), a younger Bad Dog sales person, distinguished from Greg who is older; Rao said Milani is very close to Access. Val also said he would like to talk to Rao about Puerto Rico. For the first location Val asked for a Spanish-speaking attendant, since those locations are Spanish-speaking.

## Decisions Made

- **The offer to Rao: one machine at $15,750 with fifteen-game software, and all transactional equipment free** — the machine's own port plus five additional ports, one IQ point-of-sale unit and one Pay unit, about $15,000 of value given away. (Val Levitan)
- **Conditions attached: JVL exclusive in the location, a three-year contract with the location, and Rao dedicated solely to JVL for at least two years.** Exclusivity is required because JVL cannot collect if a competing system is present. (Val Levitan)
- **Rao is in** — he said so directly when Val asked, and agreed to select five locations for October with the first installation in the first week of October. (Narasimha Rao)
- **JVL is definitely not going with MudaGoose.** (Val Levitan)
- **JVL will support both dual SAS and single SAS** — Val had no final decision entering the meeting and accepted Rao's insistence on the spot. (Val Levitan, prompted by Narasimha Rao)
- **Internal testing comes before any installation** — JVL polishes first, then installs. (JVL colleague, accepted)
- **JVL will supply a fully enclosed top-mount box on two mounting posts, with the cable run through a hollow post**, so locations with wooden cabinets or no base plate do not need cutting. (Val Levitan)
- **Val will not change JVL's market-entry approach in response to the September 2 letter** — asked directly, he said no. (Val Levitan)
- **Val wants competitor machine settings and bet-button photographs, not revenue reports** — he repeated this against Rao's offers to send the report. (Val Levitan)
- **Val will set up a JVL machine himself in the same spot in Rao's best location and let Rao test it free.** (Val Levitan)
- Reversal, and the meeting's final position: **the deal does not have to be packaged.** Val spent most of the meeting on the bundle, then ruled that a distributor can install and resell the transactional equipment standalone to operators who do not want a machine, and closed by asking the room to confirm that agreement. This supersedes the bundle-only framing used earlier in the same meeting.

## Open Questions

- Whether the deal is packaged or the transactional equipment can be sold standalone. Val reversed toward standalone and asked the room to confirm, but the closing exchange trails off without a clean answer.
- Whether an operator will sign a three-year contract. Rao's view is that an operator will not and only a location may.
- Whether Rao can find a buyer for his roughly $500,000 of Access equipment before the free offer becomes known.
- Whether the Georgia market is already too saturated to enter now. Rao thinks close to it; Val declined the premise.
- Whether the machine in Rao's location underperforms because of settings or because of its physical position. Both sides agreed to settle it by looking at the location.
- Rao's account of SkillPay as a crypto company buying the ATM-access card is explicitly his own assumption from hearsay, not verified.
