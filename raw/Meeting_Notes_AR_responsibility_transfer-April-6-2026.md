### Collections Framework and Pre-Authorized Debit (PAD)

Alka reviewed the collections framework Val had put together and expressed support for it. She highlighted JVL's existing practice of putting selected clients on pre-authorized debit (PAD) — clients provide banking details and JVL draws payments on an agreed schedule. This has been running for 6-8 months, managed by Felipe's team, using a control sheet that tracks client contracts, banking credentials, and scheduled withdrawals.

Val strongly favored expanding PAD/ACH to all customers, not just selected ones. He argued it provides mutual discipline: JVL commits to not drawing money early or late, and the customer commits to not delaying payment. It also gives JVL cash flow predictability. He called it "fantastic hygiene."

Alka proposed a tiered credit framework:
- **Tier 1 — New customers:** Mandatory PAD. "Because you're new to us, we're going to put you on a pre-authorized debit schedule."
- **Tier 2 — Customers with 2-3 years of good payment history:** May come off PAD, but receive formal credit terms with a defined credit limit.
- **Tier 3 — Long-standing customers with strong relationships:** Higher credit limits, potentially more flexible terms.

Val pushed back on removing PAD even for Tier 2 and 3 customers, asking "what is stopping us from having pre-authorized withdrawals?" even with 12-month credit limits. He argued PAD should be universal regardless of credit tier. Alka acknowledged "nothing" prevents it but proposed keeping the tiers as a negotiation framework with customers.

### Credit Assessment — Academic Approach

Val emphasized that JVL has never had a formal, academic approach to credit assessment. The company has historically extended credit informally — "he needs half a million, yeah, sounds like a good idea." Val wants to change this by introducing proper credit worthiness assessment. He drew a distinction between a company that "sounds the same" (e.g., "JVL Systems" with no assets) versus one with real assets — without proper assessment, JVL is "holding a bag of promises."

Val identified two parallel education tracks:
1. Educating Felipe on "financial management hygiene" and formal account management practices
2. Educating the accounting department on what it means academically to grant someone the "privilege of 0% financing"

### Credit Application Process

Val asked Alka to create a simplified but properly structured credit application form. Requirements discussed:
- **Bank reference** with the customer's signed consent for JVL to contact the bank directly. The application form itself should include the consent authorization.
- **Trade references** (initially Val said "three references," Alka focused on bank reference as primary).
- **Soft credit check** (Dun & Bradstreet) — Val initially proposed this, but Alka argued it's unnecessary if you have the bank reference. The bank will disclose NSFs, payment defaults, and other red flags. Val agreed: "You're probably right."

Alka committed to delivering a simple credit application form by the next day (April 7).

Val specified that the credit application must be legally enforceable in the customer's state, so it can serve as an instrument for litigation if needed. He acknowledged JVL currently has no clear path to repossessing machines or pursuing legal recourse for non-payment — the credit application is the foundation for building that path.

### Proactive Collections Process

Alka proposed a structured collections escalation process:
1. **One week before payment is due:** Laura sends a proactive email reminder — "just as a reminder, you owe us money and we're expecting to see this come in on this date." Alka compared this to how credit card companies operate.
2. **Once overdue:** Laura contacts the customer's AP department to ask what happened and requests an ETA for payment. Give them two weeks.
3. **Still unpaid after two weeks:** Laura escalates to Felipe (or the relevant salesperson) to call the customer directly.

Val endorsed this and added the concept of prefabricated notice templates (Notice 1, 2, 3) that could be scheduled and sent automatically, with Laura supervising rather than manually composing each one. Alka committed to drafting these notice templates.

### The "Bad Guy" Dynamic and Credit Check Signaling

Val articulated a deliberate strategy around the organizational dynamic for collections:
- **Felipe should not be the "bad guy"** to customers. As the salesperson/account manager, Felipe needs to position himself as the customer's ally — "I am doing everything possible to protect you from any financial hassle."
- **Finance is the enforcer.** Val plans to frame the new credit requirements as coming from a "new financial leader" who "demands credit checks." This gives Felipe cover: "I have this financial devil in my company."
- **Alka embraced this role:** "Make me the bad guy. Make me the one to phone them up." Val noted this could also be Mary or anyone in finance — the point is separating the sales relationship from the collection function.

Val argued that without formal credit checks and granted credit privileges, customers have nothing tangible to lose if they don't pay. By going through a credit application process, the customer is awarded a privilege that can be revoked — creating real leverage: "If you don't pay, your privileges will be revoked."

Val shared a personal anecdote: he recently went through a credit check himself with a company he'd dealt with for 30 years. Initially upset, he realized "they're doing it right" and wants to imitate that approach.

### Machine Economics and Default Risk

Alka asked about the economics of JVL machines to understand default risk behavior:
- A machine sells for approximately **$15,000**.
- Average daily revenue per machine is approximately **$100/day**, split roughly 55/45: the operator keeps about **$45/day**.
- At $45/day, annual operator revenue is ~$16,425 — so payback on average is about **one year**.
- However, operators buy new machines to replace their worst-performing units, and new machines perform roughly **double** the average. Actual payback for a new machine is **4-6 months**.
- JVL was historically privileged with strong demand and didn't need to offer financing. Now, **three major world-class competitors** have entered the market simultaneously, offering better terms and better performance. JVL's current sales are at zero and the company is in catch-up mode.

On default risk: Val explained that JVL has a built-in leverage mechanism — machines operate on a **lease/license timer** and require periodic renewal. If payment hasn't been received within 60 days, the machine "dies" — it becomes useless to the customer. Alka confirmed: "Then it's useless to them." Val noted the industry has a "very, very small default rate" partly because of this dynamic.

Val acknowledged that JVL currently lacks a formal fallback for worst-case non-payment scenarios. The "ugliest path" would be litigation, but the company doesn't yet have enforceable credit applications to support that. Building this is a longer-term goal.

Alka explored the customer psychology: at what point does a customer stop caring about paying? Val argued they won't — once a customer has invested 6 months of payments and the machine is profitable, they'll keep paying. Conversely, once JVL has received 50% of the machine cost, they've covered cost plus profit. The real risk is customers whose machines aren't performing as expected — they're the ones likely to default.
