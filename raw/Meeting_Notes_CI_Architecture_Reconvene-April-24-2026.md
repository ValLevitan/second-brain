## Summary

This was the Friday reconvene anticipated since the April 17 CI session. It was called to review Biratko/Bosik's draft of the v3 (subsequently v4) repository organization standard. Aaron arrived having already formed a critique. Within the first two minutes he reframed the session — rather than reviewing v4 section by section, he rewrote the standard live during the call into v5, then immediately into v5.1, and produced two companion documents afterward.

**Aaron's opening critique of v4 (6:43–9:30).** Aaron told the team directly that v4 was "extremely brittle" because it defined specific technologies (which go stale in roughly six months), not invariants. He said the document "masquerades as formalism" — it had borrowed regulatory prose from Claude, which "happily obliges because it doesn't know what it's doing," and the result sounded official but would "absolutely ruin everything you're trying to do." Aaron specifically called out v4's blanket prohibition on git submodules followed by conditional re-admission in the same document as internally confused. He committed, during the call, to rewriting the document with what he called **invariants** — things that cannot change, as distinct from variables that can. Val asked what "invariant" meant in this context; Aaron clarified that the standard should define the principles that hold regardless of which specific tools are chosen, not prescribe the tools themselves.

**v5 produced live (9:30–17:00).** Aaron generated v5 during the call with Claude. The key changes from v4: (1) a revision note at the top stating that the document now states architectural principles rather than prescribes a toolchain; (2) specific tool references (Claude, attend, AgentWays, Ways) removed from the normative sections and moved to a non-normative Appendix A labelled explicitly as swap-out-able; (3) §2 Zero Trust rewritten from first principles, anchored to NIST SP 800-207 (identity-aware proxy verifying every request at the boundary regardless of transport), with the CI repository named as the policy enforcement point — Aaron described this as "the central architectural move"; (4) v4's blanket submodule prohibition removed — submodules, image digests, and hybrids are now all acceptable as long as resolution from the integration commit to subsystem versions is unambiguous; (5) vocabulary shift from "service" to "subsystem" to generalise beyond microservice shops; (6) §6.1 code-first sharpened into a metaphor about contracts as crystallisation points; (7) §8 reframed so that bad AI output is a diagnostic signal that the repository cannot describe itself, rather than a failure of the AI. Aaron noted that he had considered adding an explicit clarification in §2 about independent verification (e.g., GLI regulatory reviewers being able to evaluate subsystems in isolation) but decided to keep the text generalised.

**v5.1 produced immediately after (17:00–27:00).** Aaron said he caught three places where v5 needed hardening and incremented to v5.1. Three new sections: **§3.1** defines what counts as a contract assertion (schema-shape verification, consumer-side contract verification, round-trip verification) with explicit exclusions (type-checking alone, a single smoke test, one-direction schema validation only) — Aaron said *"a type that compiles can still produce a runtime payload that violates the schema, because types describe intent and schemas describe wire format."* **§5.6** sets operational floors for the integration CI as a shared dependency: e2e suite under 20 min, time-to-green under 2h on daily-cadence platforms, flakiness treated as P1 incident with 24h quarantine and fix-or-remove within one release cycle, environment parity with the production reconciliation mechanism. **§6.3.1** defines the deprecation lifecycle: minimum window = longer of two platform releases or 30 calendar days. Aaron noted this is "pretty drastic compared to how you guys operate right now" — at JVL deprecation windows run to years. He emphasised the standards document describes the target state, not the as-built state: *"By me pointing out that ideally 30 days should be the time to rip and remove old stuff, you have something to compare against. You could consider that removing code from your code base is just as important as adding code."*

**Val pivots to enforcement (27:00–50:00).** Once v5.1 was produced, Val redirected the conversation. He said "up until now, we didn't get involved in how Nathan organized his repos" and asked concretely: *"Where do we start from? How do I spring this on developers who are intelligent, highly educated, willing to do work, willing to comply, but find themselves antiquated in this sort of environment?"* Val emphasised the demoralising effect of telling experienced people they have been doing things wrong. Aaron rephrased Val's framing: the question is not "how do we make the changes" — that positions developers as being in violation. The correct framing is *"this is our quality bar from this point forward; we understand every single one of your repos is out of compliance"* — the new laws apply to everyone equally, and the question becomes "how do we measure this?" Aaron walked through how each standards section maps to a starting point: missing tests → add tests; missing CI → add CI; missing CLAUDE.md → add CLAUDE.md. He emphasised this is a control-loop problem, like a thermostat — you overshoot and undershoot until you average to a working state; perfection is not achievable on the first pass.

**Code owners and the sheriff metaphor (32:00–50:00).** Val asked who would actually enforce the standard. Aaron introduced `CODEOWNERS` as a git-native concept — a file declaring the people responsible for specific parts of the code. Aaron noted JVL does not currently use CODEOWNERS. Val insisted that in Russian/Belarusian engineering culture, multi-owner models do not work — *"there's no culture of sharing a task"* — and drove Aaron to confirm that one code owner per repo is acceptable. Aaron agreed: minimum is one owner per repo. Aaron then introduced the **sheriff metaphor**: Andrei Bosik is the elected sheriff of JVL's git infrastructure; the code owners of individual repos are his deputies; the deputies uphold the sheriff's edicts and decide what may enter their repos. Aaron stressed this is game theory — the structure must make compliance the rewarded path, because otherwise standards exist on paper and nobody prioritises them against regular work.

**The Andrei dialogue — testing assumptions (33:45–40:00).** Val put Andrei on the spot asking whether Andrei would know what to do alone after this meeting. Andrei responded hesitantly. Val pressed: would Kharetski know how to continuously integrate his own repos? Andrei said yes. Would Kharetski know how to test them? Andrei said *"about this, no, I think no."* Val escalated: *"If you know how to integrate but you don't know how to test, it doesn't make sense."* Andrei qualified that developers understand unit tests but not integrated tests in full infrastructure. Val identified three categories — unit test, integration test, end-to-end test. Andrei conceded. The exchange established that the team's mental model of testing was not yet aligned with what v5.1 requires, which became one of the meeting's primary findings.

**The gate question (50:00–1:12:00).** Val returned to the central question: *"I would like to have a gate that does not let incorrect things through. Can I do it?"* Aaron confirmed yes, and walked the team toward the answer: Andrei will define the tests, run them against all repos, and produce reports per repo. Each repo will score against the metrics (e.g., "6% of 100% passing" or "50% passing"). Andrei will review these reports on a cadence (monthly, bi-weekly). Val said "that's the answer I was looking for." Aaron then asked whether Andrei knew what framework underlies all of this — the answer: **DORA** (which Andrei knew) and **CALM** (which Andrei did not know; Aaron said he would send a link). Aaron also pointed the team at **well-architected.github.com** — GitHub's high-level enterprise-scale applied-standards site — as an example of how an applied standard layer sits on top of a core standards document. Aaron's key point: the standards document is one layer; applied standards (for a Spring+Java repo, a Terraform CI repo, a TypeScript+React repo, even GitHub itself configured as code) are a separate layer, and both are needed.

**The compliance-checks and using-the-compliance-data drafts (56:00–1:29:00).** Aaron produced a third document, initially mis-versioned as "repo-standards-5.1-checks" — he clarified it is a companion document to v5.1, not a version of the standard. It contains pseudocode test cases per standards section: manifest checks, autonomy checks, unit-test presence, contract-assertion presence, and similar. Andrei asked what a governance repo would look like; Aaron said that is the structural evidence — the repo where these checks live and emit reports. Aaron described this as "looking for code smell" — detecting patterns that don't fit the standard, not fixing them automatically but flagging them for the owner to investigate. Aaron produced a fourth document, "using the compliance data," which is the thermostat/overshoot-undershoot companion: how to take the TSDB-stored measurements (Datadog, Prometheus, or any time-series data logger — Andrei's choice) and make decisions from them over time. Aaron acknowledged this makes Andrei the person responsible for watching the status and quality of every repo — he flagged this explicitly as potentially too much work for one person and asked Andrei whether he actually wanted this role. Andrei replied *"you have no choice"* — accepted.

**The enforcement escalation ladder (1:32:00–1:37:00).** Andrei asked: when his tools report 0% compliance, what does he actually do? Aaron walked through the escalation pattern: (1) "strongly worded letter" — which Andrei would draft and send; (2) when ignored, not cutting access (Aaron said this breaks the business); (3) delay product releases until quality bars are met — this requires Val to give Andrei teeth as the release gatekeeper; (4) monetary / resource cost allocation — require the violating team to spend hours or hire someone to bring compliance; (5) override — take control of the repo and make changes (dangerous because it infantilises the owner); (6) "the beatings will continue until morale improves" — meetings will continue until behaviour changes; (7) social control at small-company scale — explicit conversations that if the pattern continues, the person's place at the company is at risk. Aaron said JVL is at the dangerous intermediate scale (10–50 part-owners) where no single pattern cleanly applies. Val picked up the social-pressure angle as the most realistic for JVL: *"If we keep meeting with you and you keep ignoring us, you're influencing our happiness in the company. Maybe you should look for another job."* Aaron confirmed this is the shame pattern and is likely JVL's best lever.

**Val's closing directive (1:37:30–1:43:00).** Val closed the meeting by telling Andrei his undertaking for the next meeting is to **produce a guiding framework** — a document similar to what Biratko produced for the standard itself, but now covering how Andrei will operate as sheriff on a daily basis. The framework will then be presented by the three of them (Biratko, Bosik, Kharetski) in Minsk to collect input from the rest of the team. Val distinguished good input ("I tried it and here's how to resolve this inconvenience") from irrelevant input ("I've never worked this way and I don't think it'll work"). Val explicitly stated: **v5.1 is accepted as the standards framework.** The team is now diverting attention to the management framework that sits on top. Val also reaffirmed one code owner per repo and told the three of them to begin assigning owners across the existing repos. The next meeting is Thursday, one week out. Aaron closed by noting that people actually like being given a list of tangible things to do with clear rewards — *"people enjoy and crave the ability to have meaning and purpose in whatever they're doing. What we're defining here is the structure and architecture to have meaning and purpose."*

## Decisions Made

- **v5.1 accepted as the JVL repository-standards framework.** The Biratko-lineage (v1 → v2 → v4.2) and the Bockelie-lineage (v5.0 → v5.1) are resolved: v5.1 is the accepted form. (Decided by Val, endorsed by Aaron, not resisted by Biratko or Bosik.)
- **Specific tools remain in non-normative Appendix A only.** Claude, attend, AgentWays, Ways, ArgoCD, submodules-or-digests, CalVer — all are swap-out-able and do not belong in the normative sections. (Decided by Aaron, confirmed by Val.)
- **v4.2's blanket submodule prohibition is reversed.** Submodules, image digests, and hybrids are all acceptable linking mechanisms. The invariant is unambiguous resolution from integration commit to subsystem versions. (Decided by Aaron; Biratko did not object.)
- **Deprecation window minimum = longer of two platform releases or 30 days.** Adopted into v5.1 §6.3.1 despite this being much tighter than JVL's current pattern of years-long deprecation. (Decided by Aaron; acknowledged by Val and Andrei as the target state.)
- **Flakiness is a P1 incident.** 24h quarantine, fix-or-remove within one release cycle. v5.1 §5.6. (Decided by Aaron, accepted without objection.)
- **Andrei Bosik is the sheriff of JVL's git infrastructure.** Responsible for the overall standards enforcement system, reports, and framework. (Confirmed by Val; Andrei accepted — *"you have no choice."*)
- **Every repo has one code owner minimum (the "deputy").** Multi-owner is permitted but optional; single-owner is the floor. (Decided jointly; Val insisted on one-owner-minimum against Aaron's multi-owner default.)
- **Enforcement leans on social pressure + release control.** Not access cuts (breaks the business), not automated overrides (infantilises). Val: continued-meetings-until-behaviour-changes + explicit performance conversations is the realistic lever at JVL scale. (Decided by Val, confirmed by Aaron.)
- **Standards document framework (v5.1) and applied-standards framework (per-language / per-tech-stack) are distinct layers.** Both needed. DORA and CALM are the underlying industry frameworks; well-architected.github.com is a reference example of applied standards at enterprise scale. (Clarified by Aaron.)

## Engineering Signals

### Confirmed Signals

- **ADR — v5.1 adopted as JVL's repository-standards framework.** Supersedes v1.0, v2.0, v4.2 (Biratko lineage) and v5.0 (Bockelie intermediate). Biratko lineage preserved as historical record; v5.1 is the operative form.
- **ADR — Standards framework and applied-standards framework are separate layers.** v5.1 is the standards layer; a per-language / per-stack applied-standards layer is required above it. DORA and CALM are the underlying industry frameworks. well-architected.github.com is an external example of an applied-standards layer JVL can reference but not copy.
- **ADR — Enforcement pattern: social control + release gating, not access revocation.** Access cuts break the business; automated overrides infantilise owners. The realistic JVL lever is continued conversation plus delay-of-release authority granted by Val to Andrei, escalating to explicit "this-job-may-not-be-for-you" conversations.
- **ADR — One code owner per repo (minimum).** Multi-owner permitted but not required. Reflects JVL's cultural constraint as stated by Val.
- **CAP — Andrei Bosik takes the sheriff role.** Responsible for the governance framework, compliance reports, thermostat-style control loop, and sheriff-deputy coordination. Compensation and scope implications open.
- **CAP — Compliance data capture via TSDB.** Datadog, Prometheus, TimescaleDB, or equivalent — Andrei's choice. Time-series observations per check per platform feed dashboards and the yearly standards-revision loop.
- **CAP — `CODEOWNERS` file adopted as the JVL code-owner declaration mechanism.** Currently not in use at JVL; needs to be added to every repo.

### Suspected Signals

- **Possible REQ — Release-delay authority for the sheriff.** Aaron repeatedly referenced Andrei's need for "teeth" — formal authority delegated from Val to delay product releases when repos fail quality bars. Val did not explicitly confirm this authority during the meeting; it is implied by the acceptance of the sheriff model but not named as a written requirement. *Val, should this be formalised as an explicit delegation before Andrei's framework lands?*
- **Possible CAP — Reward mechanism for code owners.** Aaron named rewards as essential game-theory backing: compensation, role changes, or prioritisation privileges for well-owned repos. No reward mechanism is defined at JVL today. *Val, does this warrant tracking as a capability requirement on the compensation / HR side?*
- **Possible ADR — Applied-standards layer per primary tech stack.** Aaron named Spring, Terraform, TypeScript+React, and GitHub-configured-as-code as likely applied-standards buckets. This is implied by the v5.1 adoption but the actual enumeration and prioritisation of applied-standards documents is not yet decided. *Val, should this be scoped as a follow-on deliverable after Andrei's governance framework?*
