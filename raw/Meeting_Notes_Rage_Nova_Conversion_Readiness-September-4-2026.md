# Meeting Summary — RAGE_NOVA_conversion_update-September-4-2026 (Rage Nova Conversion Readiness)

**Duration:** ~88 minutes
**Attendees:** Val Levitan, Igor Metelski, Pavel Frolov, Irina Shishko, Anton Leshkevich, Evgeny Kharetski, Alexander Biratko, Dzmitry Chudzinau

## Summary

In Russian. The weekly check on converting Rage games to Nova, which turns into the wider argument about how JVL intends to develop software from now on.

**Igor Metelski's acceptance criteria.** Igor had ported the Rage game Dragon Pays to Nova and was asked to derive acceptance criteria for automated acceptance of a port. He audited what the Rage version implements against what Nova does not yet have, looking at both assets and specific classes such as the Rage state machine, then had Claude draft criteria from that audit plus the notes Claude had accumulated during the port. The result is 212 criteria grouped by area: base game, free-spin game, display of both, work with the maths server, system messages and others. He deliberately left it open whether each is automated or checked by a person. He has not yet tried handing a criterion to Claude to see whether it is ready to become a full automated test.

Pavel Frolov's suggestion was to try that, but from a clean session that knows nothing about Igor's criteria, so Claude looks at Test 360 with fresh eyes. Pavel's reasoning: if you feed Claude the criteria first, its view gets clouded and it will keep referring back to them; it might even say the current path is a dead end and propose something better. Igor accepted the approach.

Igor also raised, unprompted, that he was not clear what the team means by "prose". His working assumption was that the set of markdown files Claude produced during the port counts as prose. Nobody corrected him directly in this call.

**The standard that had not been read.** Irina asked whether Igor's work was in Git. Igor said he believed so and would double-check and commit anything missing, and mentioned moving his branch into the new repository Andrei Bosik had created. Val picked up on the word "created" and asked who decides the architecture of the repository. Pavel corrected the premise: Andrei only creates the repository name, and the content is filled in by Igor. Val pressed further on where the skeleton structure comes from, and Pavel identified the real issue: the Kaliocha repository's directory structure was built independently of what had been discussed. Val's response was short: "we have a written standard." Igor answered that he had not read it, and that he could read it and correct his work to match.

Val did not linger on it there, but returned to it at the very end of the meeting, and that closing instruction is the part worth keeping. Pavel posted the link to the standards repository in the chat, saying he did it so nobody could later claim they did not know where the standards live, and noted Andrei was supposed to have given everyone access. Val confirmed Andrei had given access to all and manually verified every one of the 39 accounts, then said: please use the standards, please add to the standards, and please point your code, as part of your routine, to read the standards first.

**Anton Leshkevich's scene validation tool.** Anton had read Igor's port notes and picked out two large gaps: launching a game from LGS with maths, and scene validation after conversion, which currently only checks that a file exists. He built a tool using Omni that loads a scene into memory, runs on Linux and Windows, and validates scenes from the command line. It is an evolution of a tool Alexander Biratko started a couple of years ago, and Anton finished it to roughly working condition this week. He asked whether it is wanted and whether people would use it.

The answers were positive from several directions. Pavel asked whether Claude can use the tool, and Anton said yes. Alexander Biratko said designers can use it during development of new games to load a scene and see it in the new engine, and possibly layer scenes to see the finished game. Alexander then pushed further: could the tool support automatic validation by taking screenshots of an original scene and comparing them after conversion. Pavel connected this to an older problem, that inside a running game with layered assets you cannot deterministically say what colour a given pixel should be, but isolating a single scene makes comparison feasible. Alexander's example was porting one symbol from the old format to the new and letting the AI compare old view against new without human involvement. Igor added that Claude already does full-cycle checks in a browser, including launching the game and taking screenshots, and that during the port he had asked it to convert a scene and compare the result against a reference screenshot. His judgement was that the tool is very useful for a human and that Claude can work without it, but that it would have helped a great deal during Kaliocha, where design hands over a JSON file and there is no way to see what was handed over.

**The pipeline Val wants.** Pavel framed the goal as: many people converting games with Claude will produce finished product quickly, but full behavioural testing in the cabinet with different switch settings will be impossible, so QA becomes the bottleneck for many months; automated checks from Claude would free QA and let JVL release faster.

Val said that was nearly right but not fully. His framing: artificial intelligence is developing very fast, in a year it will be at a different level, and in three years it may reach something close to superintelligence. If you believe that, you build the pipeline so JVL can use it at full capability. He then laid out the development flow as he understands it. Development starts with the product owner, who understands what the market needs and writes capabilities for a new product, then looks at the library of requirements already in JVL and links them to the capabilities, adding requirements where they are missing. Requirements carry acceptance criteria, which are simple single-line statements that in future will be linked to a physical line of executable code. The product owner is the bridge to engineering. Engineers read the capabilities and write features; features become feature branches in GitHub; branches generate code; code is accompanied by prose. With prose on one side and capabilities and requirements on the other, the theory is that the AI can do the work autonomously: programme the product and perform end-to-end testing, if not fully then at a large and multi-stage level whose stages add up to full coverage. Val asked directly whether anyone disagreed, and nobody did.

**Data contracts, and the argument Val closed.** Evgeny Kharetski's position is that the description linking modules should not be prose but something hard-coded and structured, living in the integration repository, with each module's data contract sitting beside its own code. Val summarised it as: more structured, and called a data contract. Pavel worried this creates extra complexity because a standard would be needed for how to link them; Evgeny answered that the standard already exists and the integration repository already works that way on IQ.

Dzmitry Chudzinau raised the harder problem: versioning after the first release. Contracts are written as OpenAPI, but the transaction repository is single, and something has to reconcile a machine in the field on one version against a server that has moved ahead. Evgeny's answer is that versions of all modules and all contracts are baked at release, so they can be restored; Pavel added that the integration repository holds a reference to a specific commit of each repository, so the contract matching the moment of release can always be pulled back. Dzmitry pushed on who then releases the transaction repository on every release, pointing out that Flex-IQ alone has twelve repositories each with its own contracts, and a wave of releases is about to start. Evgeny's position is that you release the whole product, not an individual module. Dzmitry extended the problem to the machine's contracts with CMS and the account host, which are also part of the product and would also need baking. Evgeny conceded that part is harder but thought it solvable.

Igor asked the question underneath all of it: who actually consumes the data contracts, a human or Claude, and if Claude already has the API implementation, why are the repositories not enough.

Val stopped the discussion. He said he respects the doubts, is 90 percent certain the questions disappear once the team starts actually building product, and that these should stay internal conversations about how to do it without changing the system. His ruling: JVL is not discussing whether data contracts are needed and will not decide they can be done without. The standard is written and published. He then addressed Evgeny's blocking point that nothing can move without data contracts, established that the contracts are written by Claude rather than by hand from reading the code, and reasoned that this is therefore a matter of hours or days rather than weeks. He asked everyone to confirm that each repository will carry its data contract beside the code, asked anyone who would not do it to raise a hand, and nobody did. He set the target as the end of the following week, September 11. Evgeny warned he might not make Friday because he has the largest number of contracts on one repository; Val said 80 percent would be fine and 100 percent better.

Igor raised that the maths server is a separate server and would also need a data contract, and asked how access works. Val confirmed the maths server is being opened up, saying there is almost no secrecy left in the maths. Evgeny explained the working model to Igor: Igor has inputs rather than outputs, so his contract is his input contract, and when new functionality changes contracts the two of them agree the change, each describing their own side, with zero trust in the sense that Igor does not care which service supplies the data.

**Schedule.** Val restated the plan already agreed with Irina and Pavel. September is reserved for bringing the current Omni framework to full documentation: repositories correctly organised to the standard, data contracts correctly placed in their repositories, prose finished in every repository, capabilities written and entered into Jira and closed, and requirements attached to capabilities with acceptance criteria written. He asked Pavel to lay out tasks for all the people for September so it is done by month end. From October 1 active work on the Omni framework begins. He noted that in practice October 1 has already started, since Efim rewrote his C++ maths in Rust and Anton has written parts in Rust, and Alexander reported the printer library is already being integrated into Flex-Pay. On October 1 the team presents the architecture as it then stands, matching code, prose and capabilities, and plans what functionality comes next. He added that the same must happen for the Galaxy platform, formerly CMS, which Dzmitry is working on quietly, and that JVL starts talking about Galaxy properly in October.

**Automated tests this week.** Irina described what she wants from the developer side: hooks to drop money, make a spin and so on, so tests can be written before the game exists. Igor asked whether the goal this week is to produce some automated tests at all. Val proposed the simplest visual tests that Claude can judge, working on the pretence that development had been done properly: requirements and acceptance criteria written, then code, then telling Claude that integration and unit tests have passed and now it should do end-to-end tests from the acceptance criteria, for example press a button and get a screen. Irina's example was launching the game, opening help, and checking the help matches what a capability says, with Val adding "not by eye". Pavel said Claude would do this easily and quickly, in about an hour, and Igor confirmed Claude had done this kind of thing throughout the port. Val's proposal, which Igor accepted, was to go through the 212 criteria, ask Claude to test them, and have it report where it cannot.

## Decisions Made

- **Data contracts are not up for debate. JVL proceeds with them; the standard is written and published.** Val explicitly closed the discussion and asked that it not be reopened. (Val)
- **Every repository carries its own data contract beside its code, targeted for end of the following week, September 11.** 80 percent is acceptable, 100 percent preferred. (Val, with no objection when he asked for dissent)
- **Data contracts are generated by Claude from the code, not written by hand,** which is why Val treats the task as hours or days rather than weeks. (Val, confirmed by Evgeny Kharetski)
- **The maths server is opened up and gets a data contract like everything else.** Val: there is almost no secrecy left in the maths. (Val)
- **Module linkage lives in the integration repository as structured hard-coded description, not as prose.** (Evgeny Kharetski, accepted by Val)
- **Release versions of all modules and contracts are baked at release, with the integration repository referencing specific commits,** so any past contract can be restored. Releases are of the whole product, not individual modules. (Evgeny Kharetski and Pavel Frolov; Dzmitry Chudzinau's CMS and account-host extension acknowledged as harder and left open)
- **September is for documentation of the existing Omni framework:** repositories to the standard, data contracts placed, prose finished, capabilities written and closed in Jira, requirements attached with acceptance criteria. Active Omni development starts October 1, when the architecture is presented and forward work planned. (Val, standing plan restated)
- **The same documentation exercise applies to the Galaxy platform (formerly CMS); JVL starts on Galaxy properly in October.** (Val)
- **This week's automated-test goal is the simplest visual end-to-end tests judged by Claude,** derived from acceptance criteria. (Val)
- **Igor's 212 acceptance criteria are handed to Claude to test, with Claude reporting where it cannot test.** (Val's proposal, accepted by Igor)
- **Test 360 is evaluated by Claude from a clean session that has not seen Igor's criteria,** to avoid anchoring. (Pavel Frolov, accepted by Igor)
- **Standing instruction on standards: use them, add to them, and point your code to read the standards first as part of your routine.** Access has been granted and manually verified for all 39 accounts. (Val, closing the meeting)
