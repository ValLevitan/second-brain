## Summary

A short closing segment recorded separately from the main site visit, and the more useful of the two on market structure and regulation.

**Location contracts.** Val asked how much of the Nebraska route is under contract. Rick put it above 50% but not as high as 75%, and explained the resistance: these locations *"have never had contracts. It's hard to get them into that mindset."*

**Bank Shot is dying.** Val had seen a countertop unit on a location floor and asked what it was. Rick explained the structure: **American Amusement** owns the **Bank Shot** software and sells only the board and computer, not cabinets — an operator buys empty shells, installs the components, and builds the machines in its own shop. Any cabinet can become a Bank Shot unit, including a Banilla cabinet, just by swapping the board. The games look like 1980s–90s five-reel slots ("Mister and Misses" among them) and run on a progressive prize pool: operators pay a fee per machine based on amount played, a small share of every bet feeds the pool, and when a player hits it the operator hand-pays immediately and American Amusement reimburses what was already paid in.

Bank Shot's position was built on a legal win, not a product — American Amusement sued the State of Nebraska and won, which no other manufacturer would attempt, and that gave them the right to place machines statewide, including gas stations. For years they were the only thing available in Nebraska and effectively held a monopoly. Banilla's arrival broke it, and other manufacturers have eroded it since. Rick's verdict on the equipment now: one of J&J's worst performers, kept alive by a small group of loyalists who track jackpot levels through an app and travel to play, and *"I've probably taken more bank shots out this year."* He sees no path back — the owner *"doesn't want to update his software. He's basically moving himself out of the market."* Val asked whether progressives still matter in Nebraska; Rick said not as much anymore — what matters now is the player being able to see what the win is on site.

**The Nebraska withholding problem.** This is the segment's substance. Rick contrasted the two states he knows. In Illinois the licence runs through the lottery and nothing above the taxable threshold can be won on a single spin, so machines are capped at **$1,199** per spin because $1,200 is taxable. Nebraska has no such cap — a player can win **$5,000 on one spin**, and the operator is then expected to obtain a W-9. Rick asked Val directly whether JVL's machines could handle this the way **Easy Links** does, with what he called casino software that lets the player complete the W-9 at the terminal so J&J does not have to send someone out to pay the player and collect their details.

Worse is coming: Nebraska is moving to require operators to check winners against a **state database of people who owe state taxes or support**, and to withhold what is owed from the payout. Rick expects his legal team is trying to stop it and was frank about the consequence — *"you imagine how pissed off people are going to be with that?"* Val named it: *"you become a policeman of the state."*

This is the same rule MG Oil raised two days later in South Dakota, where the workaround under discussion was to configure machines so they never trigger a hand pay ([[Meeting_Notes_MG_Oil_Dana_Giesey_South_Dakota_Visit-June-18-2026]]). Val's answer to MG Oil — split a payout above the threshold into two smaller ones — is the direct response to Rick's question here, and it makes terminal-side W-9 capture and hand-pay avoidance a **product requirement for Nebraska**, not a nice-to-have.

**Other detail.** J&J is about 70 employees nationally by Rick's count. JVL is also dealing with an operator headquartered in South Dakota with a small Nebraska presence — MG Oil, whom Val was visiting two days later — and Val noted that JVL's amusement business has historically serviced every state: at its peak it supported about **50% of the entire US countertop market**, touching every operator in the country, though that was a couple of decades ago. Val was flying to South Dakota the next day and on to Canada. Rick asked after Dennis, who had been in Nebraska and had just left for Florida.
