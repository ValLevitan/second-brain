# Meeting Summary — Gaming Industry Trade Show Report
**Date:** April 2, 2026, 7:37 PM Eastern
**Duration:** ~15 minutes
**Segment:** Segment 2 of 2
**Attendees:** Val Levitan, Michael Gottlieb, Lena Zhukova, Juan Martinez, Efim Podvoiski

## Summary

Michael Gottlieb reported on his recent visit to the Indian Gaming trade show, sharing market intelligence gathered from conversations with senior executives at major gaming companies. He prefaced his report by saying he was "submitting without comment or agenda."

**Zitro** has crossed the $50 million mark in investment in North American slot machine development and production — covering headcount, facilities, games, and related costs. Despite shipping numerous games, they only recently achieved their first game earning house average. The company is run by Johnny Ortiz (whom Val knows personally). Derek, the North American head who Michael used to work for, is still commuting from Hawaii. Michael presented Zitro's $50M spend for one house-average game without explicit commentary, but the subtext was clear: game development in this market is extraordinarily expensive and success is not guaranteed.

**Ainsworth/Novamatic deal has collapsed.** Both companies are now "deer in the headlights." Keith, who runs Ainsworth, told Michael they had been operating entirely under the auspice of the deal — backing off development, letting people go, making strategic cuts. Now they have to figure out their independent paths with significantly depleted capabilities. Ainsworth has "nothing that's working at all in the market right now." Kathy at Novamatic, who now runs all of North America, is frustrated — she's been through the cycle of receiving product from the German headquarters that doesn't resonate in the North American market. She had been looking forward to the Ainsworth partnership to get North American-centric product. Her booth at the show was uninspiring.

**Konami** is in serious trouble. They have "nothing that's working in the market." Michael saw Tan Tan Jingoli putting on a game face. The company is cutting staff aggressively — Jason Stage (who ran the Scottsdale studio for Michael at Bally and had been at Konami) was recently let go after his game didn't meet "incredible thresholds." Michael described the cuts as "hacking and whacking and slashing, kind of indiscriminately."

**Light & Wonder (IGT/Everi merger)** fired 1,000 people, and this is only phase one — more rounds of cuts are expected before Gene Venneman (brought in for integration) is done. Jamie Knight was promoted to Senior VP underneath Gene. She told Michael the biggest operational problem is that nobody was told who was on the termination list — she's trying to call people and getting bounced emails because they've been fired without her knowing. Despite the chaos, products that are still working: Ultimate Fire Link, Lion Link, Hot Hot Flaming Pots, Willy Wonka, and they're seeing "initial good numbers" on a new Lobster Mania (a revived IGT classic). Key talent has left: Sean Seung, the main math wizard, was promoted to VP and quit two months later (couldn't take the politics — just wants to make games); George Bouvier, another top mathematician, went to IGT. Kim Collin at Light & Wonder put on a game face but the organization is hollowed out. She's been through 9 presidents in her career there.

**Aristocrat** is the market leader but under extreme internal pressure. Joe Kabenkow (whose father was friends with Michael's father in the business) described the pressure as "unlike anything I've ever experienced." MO Mummy Spooky Link has been "spectacular" for them and Lightning Storm continues to perform. However, an Aristocrat executive (the Director of Global Product Marketing Strategy, formerly at Bally with Michael) was candid about their challenges: customers are demanding 4x house average performance to justify purchasing new product — a threshold she described as impossible. She acknowledged that Aristocrat is known to game the Eilers report rankings by forcing casinos to place their products in the highest-volume heat map locations. Even with this advantage, they're struggling to sell new product. A litany of expensive game failures was listed: Master Magician, Goldilocks, Lu Long Men, Thunder, NFL, Ruby's Wild Weekend, Triple Piggy Treasures, Philip Express — "millions of dollars out, sunk to the bottom of the ocean." The development team gets whipsawed between "do something completely new and innovative" and "just do another Buffalo iteration." When Sega Sammy won a single bank at a casino, it triggered a crisis at Aristocrat. They are "fighting for fraction of a percent" of market share while trying to meet aggressive shareholder projections they don't know how to hit.

**Market-wide trends**: The Nevada market is deteriorating. Senior executives from all major players attended the show in unusual numbers, courting Native American casinos because tribal gaming is where the money is — flights and hotels have become so expensive that consumers are staying local and driving to Indian casinos, whose numbers continue to climb. Juan confirmed that several Florida casinos missed their February numbers, and operators are responding by trimming premium products (one casino completely removed all Wheel of Fortune machines; Lightning Link and Dragon Link reductions at $50-60/day savings). Both IGT/Everi and Light & Wonder have delisted from US stock markets (shifting to Australia), allowing them to operate with less public disclosure — Juan noted this means they "can now do whatever they want." Apollo's impending involvement in the merged entity adds further uncertainty.
