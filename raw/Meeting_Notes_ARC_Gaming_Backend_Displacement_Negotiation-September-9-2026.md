## Summary

A short, largely social conversation that turned into Val disclosing his real strategic intent and asking for the executive's account.

**Opening.** The executive is close to closing a sale, is buying a house in St. Petersburg, Florida on the Intracoastal, and has another business in Chicago where he once had one of the largest outlets before selling it. He holds some equity but says most of the operations are privately owned. Both men discussed having attorneys in the family.

**Bill.** A recurring figure. The executive and Bill have been partners, he has known Bill since the 1990s, and Bill now runs a kiosk company — supplying the executive's kiosks. Bill's kiosk dispenses gift cards and he is adding another product. Val was complimentary, saying Bill's kiosk is fantastic and he has done a great job, and the executive said Bill is responsive: he can call and get anything changed on the kiosk.

Val used Bill as the honesty test, twice. He asked directly whether he would be stepping on Bill's toes, and the executive said "a little bit, yeah". Val then asked the sharper question — aside from the kiosks, he is looking at taking the business away from InComm, and is Bill doing anything of that nature. The executive said Bill is looking at different things and is working on a solution. Val's position: if all Bill has done is the kiosks, and they are already paid for, then there is business to discuss; if Bill is on it, Val said he should not be there. He said he was not convinced either way. The executive replied he is not against what Val is proposing.

**Class A.** The executive said Class A is not doing great, but attributed that to other causes and said the cash cards coming out are improving payoffs. In his account the only real difference from Class B is the meters — you put twenty dollars in and have to play it all off — while the game content on screen can be very similar. He said they are putting machines in places that probably should not have them, "slinging them out and seeing what sticks", but believes there is a market and they have picked a large group they think could work. Commercially he does not buy the cabinets: it is a revenue share, a percentage to the manufacturer, and where JVL's own cabinet is used the percentage is lower than if the manufacturer's cabinet is used. Val worked through the alternatives out loud — zero up front on pure revenue share, versus a thousand dollars up front plus twenty percent, versus four thousand up front plus ten. The executive noted a Class A licence is much easier to get than a Class B, which is part of why they are doing it, and that some of their people are getting licensed for both. Val asked for a handshake deal on engineering it; the executive's answer was that it is not hard to engineer, it is just the meter. Val agreed and said JVL has done much harder things. On the regulatory horizon the executive put legislative change five years away and said it is easier to play defence than offence.

**Cash-card economics, from the operator's side.** He walked the maths: the new card costs about two dollars; taking it to an ATM adds a three-dollar fee plus another two-fifty, so the player is out about eight-fifty. ATM fees are unavoidable unless he owns the ATM himself, in which case he would charge about a dollar. He is looking for ways to cut that, and said he would rather give up his own share of the fee and hand it back to the customer, because charging less than the next operator is his competitive advantage — "I'll give up three dollars to gain twenty."

**The size of the prize.** The executive did the arithmetic on the backend himself: two dollars on the card, four-fifty made on the transaction in the backend, 47,000 machines in the market, five cash-outs per machine per day is about 235,000 cash-outs a day; controlling eighty percent gives 188,000 a day at five-fifty, which he called a million dollars. He said it is a great business model and asked how quickly Val could get there. Val's answer: if a partner's solution is used it is available now, but he is not sure that partner is the right solution; if JVL has to build it itself, half a year.

**Val's offer and his intent.** JVL has a machine that is making money and is price-competitive at $15,750. With it he gives away a kiosk, a point of sale and five more ports that go into the other machines — converting the whole location. He described the kiosk as a slim unit that fits between machines: e-wallet, bill acceptor, card dispenser and printer, with no cash dispensing and no recycling, and the wallet moving from machine to machine. A JVL colleague explained the FlexPort as an AFT device that fits an AFT window and connects to the SAS 2 port, emulating an AFT device — and stated the constraint plainly: JVL must be exclusive in the location or it does not work for them. With five ports plus the JVL machine that is six machines, about the location average.

Val then disclosed the real objective, prefacing it as highly confidential and noting there is no confidentiality agreement in place but that he was trusting a senior executive: on the back end JVL is looking to replace InComm. He said George does not know, that the executive is the only person in the industry who knows, and that he was telling him because winning his account would be a big deal.

**Where it landed.** The executive said Val cannot buy his entire business and asked how much of it he could get. He noted he has other point-of-sale systems in the field besides Bill's. Val said he has already made a lot of deals and would like this one, and that he would love to see one machine per location with JVL's kiosk, point of sale and ports. They agreed to meet again — Wednesday the 16th, at the executive's place. Val is back Monday night and travelling to Miami.

## Decisions Made

- **The offer as stated: the JVL machine at $15,750, with a kiosk, a point of sale and five additional ports given away**, converting the whole location. (Val Levitan)
- **JVL must be exclusive in the location or the FlexPort arrangement does not work** — stated as a hard requirement by the JVL colleague, not negotiated. (JVL)
- **The kiosk has no cash dispensing and no recycling** — e-wallet, bill acceptor, card dispenser and printer only, with the wallet portable between machines. (Val Levitan)
- **Val disclosed JVL's intent to replace InComm on the back end**, framed as highly confidential and shared with no one else in the industry, and specifically not with George. (Val Levitan)
- **Next meeting set for Wednesday, September 16, at the executive's place.** (Both)
- **Timeline if JVL builds the backend itself: half a year.** An existing partner's solution is available now but Val is not convinced it is the right one. (Val Levitan)
- **Exclusivity was sought and NOT obtained.** Val confirmed on 2026-09-10 that his attempt to secure exclusivity failed and that **JVL will not have all of Sloan's business** — the "verbal handshake" he described in the 5:31 PM debrief an hour later was a matter of speech, not an agreement. (Val Levitan, corrected 2026-09-10)
- **Val's own assessment of this meeting is positive** — the broader agenda of winning the ARC account is working, even though exclusivity did not. (Val Levitan, 2026-09-10)
- Considered and left open: **whether Val proceeds at all given the executive's relationship with Bill.** Val twice offered to withdraw — "if Bill is on it and you're friends with Bill, then I shouldn't be here" — and said he was not convinced. The executive said he is not against Val's proposal, but nothing was settled.
