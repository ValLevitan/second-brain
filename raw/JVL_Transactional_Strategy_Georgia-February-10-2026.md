# JVL Transactional Equipment Strategy — Georgia

**Sources:** _Diamond Games / Shawn Fellows meeting (Feb 9, 2026); Val Levitan / Juan Martinez call (Feb 10, 2026)_

## The Core Concern: Operator Trust

### Shawn Fellows (Diamond Games) — Feb 9

During the strategic partnership meeting, Shawn was direct: master operators will not be comfortable with JVL, a slot machine manufacturer, going directly to retail locations. These are the operator’s customers, and Shawn stated he personally would not want a manufacturer engaging with his retail accounts. The master operator relationship must flow through the operator’s own team.

However, Shawn left one opening. He acknowledged a potential service model where JVL could offer turnkey deployment and management as a service to smaller operators who lack internal staff to administer transactional systems—provided the master operator is involved from day one and explicitly approves the arrangement.

Shawn also confirmed that since these systems connect to COAMS (coin-operated amusement machine systems), master operator involvement is effectively mandatory for proper integration regardless.

### Val Levitan / Juan Martinez — Feb 10

Val acknowledged this concern as real and legitimate, likely shared by every operator in Georgia, not just Shawn. The key insight from this discussion centers on perception. InComm successfully went directly to over 4,500 locations with their handheld gift card device because operators perceive InComm as a financial services company—like a bank—not a competitor in the gaming business. JVL, as a slot machine manufacturer, does not have that luxury. Going directly to locations would create the perception that JVL is reaching into operators’ territories and gaining visibility into their business operations that a manufacturer shouldn’t have.

Val stated the need to be “humble about it and try to overcome, not to be cocky about it” when it comes to the data access JVL would gain through transactional systems.

## The Strategy: Go Through the Operator, Not Around Them

Based on both conversations, JVL’s distribution model is built around a single principle: respect the operator’s territory. JVL goes to market _through_ master operators, not directly to locations. The economic model is designed to give operators genuine incentive to participate rather than resist.

### 1. POS Device — Credit-Based Pricing

The POS device retails at approximately $1,000. Operators pay the full $1,000 (an actual transaction, not a wash), but immediately receive a $1,000 credit note applicable against any future JVL purchase—machines, parts, anything. The net effect is that the POS is essentially free to the operator, but the credit mechanism pulls them back into JVL’s ecosystem for future purchases.

Val discussed packaging this credit note as a serialized, premium-looking metal card in a high-quality box to make it feel tangible and valuable. Each credit note would carry a unique folio number entered into JVL’s system against the operator’s account.

### 2. Recurring Revenue Share — $0.20 Per Card Activation

Master operators receive $0.20 per card activation as ongoing revenue. This gives them a financial stake in the success of the transactional system and an active reason to promote it across their locations. Val noted this also serves as a price discovery mechanism: if InComm is paying operators more than $0.20, operators will say so immediately once JVL makes the offer, surfacing competitive intelligence.

### 3. The Security Sales Pitch

Juan identified JVL’s strongest competitive differentiator against InComm: security. InComm’s handheld device does not interact with Mother Goose or the gaming system at all—it is entirely manual, which creates a theft opportunity at the location level. JVL’s solution integrates directly with the gaming system, removing that manual gap. This is positioned as the lead selling point when speaking with operators.

### 4. Product Lineup — Tiered to Fit Different Needs

The strategy offers operators a menu of products rather than a one-size-fits-all approach:

- **POS device (~$1,000):** Simple card dispensing terminal for locations. Full credit note issued against future JVL purchases.
- **FlexPort (~$1,000 retail, ~$300 net after credit):** A simplified screen-based device, potentially without a printer, using phone-based QR codes instead of paper tickets. Priced at $1,000 with $700 in JVL credit, making the out-of-pocket cost approximately $300. Val preferred the credit model over a straight discount because accumulated credits pull operators back for future purchases.
- **FlexPay Lite kiosk:** A stripped-down, inexpensive kiosk with a screen and possibly a validator—no printer—relying on QR codes sent to players’ phones. Eliminates paper servicing headaches entirely.
- **Full kiosk / ATM configuration:** The current expensive kiosk form factor only makes economic sense if it evolves into a cash-dispensing ATM with bill-to-bill recyclers. Development on this is progressing for the Texas market with a 22-inch-wide recycler unit designed to fit between two gaming machines.

The core message to operators is that printers and bill acceptors become optional convenience items, not requirements. This eliminates significant ongoing maintenance costs (paper jams, mechanical service, location garbage) that operators currently deal with.

### 5. Accounting and Service Data — Handled with Care

Through dual-SAS ports available on all machines, JVL can offer operators real-time machine performance data pushed directly to their phones—a significant value-add compared to the current process of manually pulling Intralot reports. Shawn himself noted during the Feb 9 meeting that operators currently rely on Intralot reports that cannot be easily tied back to specific locations or game-level performance.

However, both Val and Juan acknowledged the sensitive reality: this capability means JVL would know a great deal about operator locations and revenue—more than a manufacturer typically should. Val raised this directly, saying “there will be an issue, somewhat of an issue where JVL knows too much.”

Two approaches were discussed to mitigate this concern:

1. **Local data only:** Make the data remain local to the operator, with JVL having no access to it. As Juan put it, “The data remains locally for them to use.”
2. **Third-party verified Chinese wall:** Create a formally isolated data environment with independent third-party verification and certification proving JVL cannot access operator-specific performance data.

Juan noted that in the Class III casino world, it is understood that system manufacturers can dial in and view game performance data—it is “frowned upon yet accepted.” He believes the same dynamic could work in Georgia’s distributed route market, provided JVL positions the data as being for the operator’s benefit.

### 6. Credit Over Discount — Building Long-Term Loyalty

A consistent theme across all product pricing is the preference for JVL credit over straight discounts. Val articulated the logic clearly: “The more credit they accumulate, they will come back and buy something from us.” A $300 discount is a one-time event that ends the relationship. A $700 credit note is an open loop that brings the operator back to JVL’s door for their next purchase.

## Summary

JVL’s transactional equipment strategy for Georgia is built around one central principle: respect the operator’s territory. JVL goes to market through master operators, not directly to locations. The economic model—full credit on POS devices, recurring per-transaction revenue share, and credit-based pricing on all hardware—gives operators genuine financial incentive to participate. The security integration with the gaming system and optional accounting data services add differentiated value that InComm’s manual handheld solution cannot match. The data privacy safeguards—whether through local-only storage or a certified Chinese wall—address the trust concern that naturally arises when a manufacturer gains visibility into an operator’s business.

Val concluded the Feb 10 call by asking Juan to percolate on the model and reconvene, expressing confidence that “this is a good model.”
