## Summary

The follow-up to the August 24 prose checkpoint. Vladimir Samusenko reported what he had built: drawing on the meetings, the discussions, the work he, Evgeny and Anton had done and the prose written for LGS, he assembled a single prose standard that satisfies everything the team had been finding and doing.

The call opened with a sharp correction. Val asked whether the standard was already in GitHub; Vladimir said it currently sits in the LGS repository, since LGS is the pilot project for prose and most experiments ran there. Val objected in strong terms — he said the approach irritates him to the core, that he keeps failing to break it, and apologised for the tone while insisting the point stands. His rule: if a standard exists at all, even a weak or unfinished one, it has exactly one location. It cannot sit off to the side in another repository because that is convenient for someone. It goes where standards go, and yes it will develop and change. He named the "standards" repository; Pavel corrected him that the repository is called `knowledge` and standards is a folder inside it, and Val accepted the correction with thanks. Vladimir agreed to move the standard there, subject to having write access, which he probably does not; Pavel took on writing to Andrei to grant company-wide access to the knowledge repository, and Irina offered to send Vladimir the link.

**What Vladimir built.** Beyond the standard itself, the valuable output is two Claude Code skills. The first initialises prose for a project that never had any: it studies the repository's structure and dependency tree (not its architecture — he was precise about this), using whatever file describes structure, a POM for a backend or a routing file listing screens for a frontend, so it can see the project as a tree spreading from a single starting point. From those dependencies it proposes a mapping — which unit prose will be divided into, a module for a backend, a screen or component for a frontend, something else for other projects, since the unit cannot be fixed in advance. It asks the developer clarifying questions: dead code it found that may not need describing, service components, tests, helper functionality that does not bear on the prose. Once agreed it writes an index file recording the areas (frontend, backend, common) and, per area, the mapping of which prose covers which files — and, importantly, it keeps in that same document the context of the conversation with Claude: what to throw out, what not to look at, what to focus on. After the developer reviews and accepts that document, Claude walks the plan and writes prose component by component. At this stage capabilities may not exist yet; the skill accounts for that and marks them TBD rather than ignoring them, so nothing is missed. Where capabilities do exist, Claude can be asked to link the prose to them — Vladimir did this on the Recycler branch and got roughly 80–95% coverage, with a couple of general files that could not map to any capability. He and Alexander checked several capabilities and found them truthfully matched to the prose that relates to them.

The second skill, called simply "prose", handles ongoing change. Vladimir described the update mechanism as the hardest part to organise, because two developers can touch the same prose. His solution: the developer does not write prose directly into the prose file. The skill produces an artifact — a request to change the prose — and those artifacts land in a `pending` folder, travelling with the pull request into the branch. After code review, once the team is happy with how the prose reads, a GitHub action fires on the merge to main and merges the artifacts into the main prose. The point is that nobody overwrites anyone: if Evgeny merged a change three and a half seconds earlier, Vladimir cannot silently wipe it. If the action cannot combine the text it fails, a separate branch is created, and the team merges by hand — losing nothing. Vladimir named the real danger plainly: the core risk with prose is failing to write something and then never knowing it was missing.

LGS is now fully documented, and the skills were also tested successfully on the Recycler. LGS produced 54 capabilities; the Recycler, a smaller project, 34. Irina pushed back that 54 is too granular and that what is written are not capabilities — "issue and reprint a paper voucher" or "redeem an inserted voucher" are not capabilities in her view. Pavel countered that LGS is genuinely a huge project and 50 might be normal for it. Val's answer was procedural rather than architectural: when he wrote prose himself (correcting himself that Claude wrote it, "let us call things by their proper names") he also got too many capabilities the first time, went back and explained to Claude how JVL works administratively — that capabilities sit at a fairly high level, that he expected to describe the product in around twenty capabilities, with roughly eight requirements and about eight features per capability, plus or minus. Claude then structured it very conveniently. He told the team they can give Claude exactly that kind of administrative direction — described as an administrative approach, not an architectural one — and consolidating 54 is five minutes of work.

**Why prose at all.** Evgeny Kharetski raised the honest question: going one direction is fine and helps derive capabilities, but once code is written to capabilities and prose written to that code, what is it for? He said it currently looks as though the prose just lies there, and the team should discuss what can be squeezed out of it — help with autotests, perhaps — before committing. Val answered with two reasons, the first and most important being to make automated end-to-end tests possible. He laid out the correct order for the business: requirements arrive from the market, capabilities are born from them, existing requirements are reused and connected to capabilities, new ones written; so a product might carry twenty capabilities with around eight requirements each, roughly eighty requirements, and no code yet. Development then produces features closing each requirement, and at commit time the end-to-end tests run, built point-by-point on each requirement's acceptance criteria. If prose sits properly next to the code, Claude can run those tests reliably against the acceptance criteria. Vladimir added technical support for this: he used an IRS-style state-description approach in the prose, one of the best practices for documentation — the prose is broken into blocks stating that if the state is X then Y happens, with figures where needed, which is very close to what an autotest needs. Evgeny said that if Val had not given that answer he would have given it himself, and Val was pleased the question had been raised.

Val then asked whether the team was united on the approach and got agreement. Evgeny noted that developers would be signed up regardless because the repository will be configured to require prose; Val disagreed with that framing — you cannot force people, and if someone simply will not do it, that person probably does not fit the collective. If everyone genuinely finds the approach convenient and right, he does not understand why any of the normal people who work at JVL would refuse.

**The Val–Irina disagreement.** Discussing how to handle the existing capabilities and requirements, Val's view was that the code in main is written, its capabilities are therefore closed, and what remains open is requirements with acceptance criteria — and the team should not stop at capabilities but tell Claude that requirements and acceptance criteria must accompany them, written narrowly and pointwise, one test per acceptance criterion. Irina objected on the ground that code being written does not mean it is verified. Val held that the code is committed and in the main branch and is nominally checked. He went further: because the old requirements were written against Rage, not Nova, the requirements may match but the acceptance criteria certainly should not, and they will not bind to the code. Irina disagreed — whether Rage or Nova, the machine has a game, takes money and pays money out, so the requirement remains, merely implemented in another language. Val's counter was that acceptance criteria must fit narrowly to the specific code sitting in the repository today. Irina said she does not understand the purpose of tying an acceptance criterion to a piece of code: the team should write a test that checks the acceptance criteria, and if something is not verified it either cannot be, or is not implemented, or is implemented wrongly — and that is exactly how you find the problem. If instead you derive acceptance criteria from the code, everything will always agree and always look correct.

Val conceded. He said he may be reaching the point where he lacks the practical knowledge, and proposed settling it by experiment: take a piece of code, its prose, capabilities and some requirements, and produce end-to-end tests — for instance the existing requirements to test coin-in and a counter — and see how Claude copes with writing the test. He also questioned whether the task had been framed correctly for Igor Metelski, who might approach it crudely, now that the team has worked out in detail what is needed. Pavel added a practical caution: there are many requirements in Jira, but it would be good if one percent are written properly with acceptance criteria in the description, because only in the last month and a half has anyone started writing them in that format. Val thought Claude could fix that fairly easily. Pavel also noted this reopens the earlier discussion of whether requirements live forever; Val corrected the record — the team said they live a long time, not forever, and they did live long, about twenty years for Rage, with JVL only starting to write them two years ago and nominally catching up eighteen prior years.

Assignment: Pavel proposed Alexey Khamin, and Ivan as capacity allowed once South Dakota and Georgia ship. Val said one person, not two — Khamin.

**The gap Evgeny found.** Pavel described what should happen mechanically: with prose and requirements carrying acceptance criteria, tests get generated with Claude in the integration repository, a registry of the generated tests is kept and updated with each build, and on a build the system analyses which pull requests changed the ISO, which capabilities and features they touch, whether tests exist for that functionality, running them if so and proposing to extend them if incomplete.

Evgeny said writing a test is not the hard part — the hard part is the new test harness, because there is a lot that is new, particularly separate services, and to test properly you have to bring up, for example, the LGS game plus a bill-validator service, since LGS talks to the validator and the game must display the right counters. Then he named the real blockers. There are no contracts — for instance no contract for the new validator service to interact with LGS, and no contract between LGS and the game. And there is no integration repository for Nova at all: one exists for IQ, but not for the machine-side of Nova. He expects the integration repository to reference all the games, LGS, the services (bill validator, printer and so on), possibly the math — and none of it exists.

Val called this a very good point and thanked Evgeny for raising it, then asked how to create the contracts and the repository — checking that contracts are written by Claude and not by hand, which Evgeny confirmed as the right approach. Pavel and Irina worked through what exists: Alexander had built a way to assemble a game and run it on a machine, but Pavel pointed out that is the old approach with a hardware layer as a set of libraries, whereas Evgeny is talking about everything being split into Rust modules, each providing separate functionality that must be integrated with the others — and for that nothing exists. Evgeny added the plain constraint: an integration repository without contracts will not work.

The work was then divided. Anton Rishkevich's five or six Rust modules each need prose plus capabilities plus a contract. Evgeny will take the LGS contracts, counting four or five entry points — devices, the game, SAS, and external servers and player control — and noted math also runs through LGS, though LGS only relays data without processing it. Anton writes the contracts for the device side, which Evgeny called the easy part and best done by him. Someone must do the same from the games side; Irina suggested Anton, Igor, or Danil, and Evgeny thought Danil might be better, with Irina noting she had promised Danil the previous day that he would be given real work. On the integration repository itself: Andrei creates it, and populating it will likely involve several people committing at once — everyone connected to the contracts. Contracts live in their own repositories with the integration repository referencing them. Pavel expected the integration repository to need prose in which people describe in words that there is a link between, say, the local game server and the printer module; Evgeny disagreed, arguing it should be more technical files, something like JSON, so the connection is always precise — declared the way you declare a variable in code — otherwise Claude may drift from what was intended. Irina supported this: it must be clear that Claude goes to the printer and is not suddenly carried off to the bill validator. Pavel accepted the shape: rules stating that a given JSON describes all module links, with Claude taking references to other repositories from a `docs/contracts` folder to understand which interfaces it receives data through, and the tests then built from each repository's capabilities and requirements with the inter-module contracts taken into account. Evgeny expects two skills eventually — one working backwards to write tests for what already exists, and one that looks at a new feature or integration, pulls the pull requests across repositories carrying the same capability, sees what changed and proposes and writes the tests.

Vladimir added one more item: he had spoken with Andrei the previous day and they agreed that once he finishes, the skills move into the Claude Code configuration so every developer uses the same version and everyone receives updates — and Val added that the standard updates with them.

**Targets and close.** Val set out his expectation: by October 1, code, prose, capabilities and requirements for the Nova system modules are 90% done. He asked directly whether that is realistic and asked to be told yes, because otherwise his plans collapse. Pavel said it is unlikely they close 100%, and doubted even 50% of tests by October. Val agreed on tests specifically and put the figure between 10 and 40%, adding that something tells him it will go significantly faster than expected — either it goes very fast and is nearly done within a week, or the team is doing something wrong. Pavel's concern was the adequacy of the requirements: capabilities will be ready and that is not hard, but each requirement must be read by a human, not simply generated by Claude and skimmed afterwards, and that is the long pole. Irina raised the parallel need to move the tidied capabilities into Jira and build the new Jira structure, LGS possibly being the next project after the work Dima started before his holiday; Val said she had put it too softly — he sees it as impossible without.

Val cut off further theorising and asked for work to be distributed so time is not lost. Two milestones over the next two weeks were fixed: the contracts, and the integration repository created and populated. Evgeny was candid that with his current load he is not certain he will finish everything by the end of next week but will do the maximum, and that only after contracts and the repository can autotest work begin. Val closed by expanding the next day's meeting (September 4) to include Vladimir, Evgeny, Dima and Anton Rishkevich, said task ownership would be polished there, left Pavel to assign all the tasks, and set the next check-in for Friday at 08:00. He thanked Vladimir, called Evgeny's intervention a good input and Irina's a good comment, and described the meeting as very productive.

## Decisions Made

- **The prose standard moves out of the LGS repository into the `knowledge` repository's standards folder.** Val's rule: a standard has exactly one location even when unfinished, and it develops there. (Val, corrected on the repo name by Pavel)
- **Company-wide access to the `knowledge` repository to be granted.** Pavel to write to Andrei. (Pavel)
- **Prose is not edited directly.** The skill emits change artifacts into a `pending` folder that travel with the pull request; a GitHub action merges them into the main prose on merge to main, failing loudly rather than losing information when it cannot combine text. (Vladimir Samusenko)
- **The prose skills move into the Claude Code configuration** so every developer runs the same version and receives updates; the standard updates alongside them. (Vladimir with Andrei; Val added the standard)
- **Capabilities are consolidated by giving Claude an administrative direction** — roughly twenty capabilities per product, about eight requirements and eight features each — rather than accepting the 54 generated for LGS. Val framed this as an administrative, not architectural, instruction. (Val)
- **The purpose of prose is to make acceptance-criteria-driven end-to-end tests possible.** Confirmed as the agreed rationale after Evgeny challenged it; the team signed up to the approach. (Val, endorsed by Evgeny, Pavel, Irina, Vladimir)
- **Contracts are required for all modules, and are written by Claude rather than by hand.** (Evgeny raised, Val confirmed the method)
- **An integration repository for the machine side of Nova must be created and populated** — one exists for IQ but not for Nova. Andrei creates it; contracts live in their own repositories and the integration repository references them. (Evgeny, Pavel, Irina)
- **Module links in the integration repository are expressed as technical files (e.g. JSON), not prose**, so the connections stay precise and Claude cannot drift. (Evgeny, supported by Irina, accepted by Pavel)
- **Alexey Khamin — one person, not two — takes the work of attaching requirements to capabilities and auditing acceptance criteria against the code.** (Val, on Pavel's proposal)
- **Two milestones for the next two weeks: the contracts, and the integration repository created and populated.** (Evgeny, agreed by Val)
- **Val conceded the acceptance-criteria disagreement to an experiment** rather than ruling: take code, prose, capabilities and existing requirements (e.g. coin-in, a counter) and see how Claude writes the end-to-end test. (Val, after Irina's objection)
- **Targets set:** by October 1, code/prose/capabilities/requirements for Nova system modules 90% done; end-to-end tests between 10 and 40% (Pavel: certainly under 50%). (Val and Pavel)
- **Tomorrow's meeting (September 4) expanded** to include Vladimir, Evgeny, Dima and Anton Rishkevich, where task ownership is finalised; next check-in Friday 08:00. (Val)
