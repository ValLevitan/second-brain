## Summary

A long, wandering strategy conversation — mostly Val and the games lead, in a car and then at a machine — covering five subjects.

**1. Naming the transactional business.** The games lead proposed **JTE — the JVL Transactional Ecosystem**. Val liked the concept and rejected the abbreviation, asked for a different one, and tried several out loud ("JVL enrichment", "Jet"). **Unresolved.** Val also said he does not want gaming machines folded into the ecosystem at all: the machine business is creative and difficult and he wants it kept separate from what he called the mechanical transactional business.

**2. What Val is actually chasing: the float.** He set out the economics plainly. About 1% on the transacted amount, but the real prize is the money that is left behind and never processed — he put that at roughly 8%. His stated goal is to become a partner at a bank and hold that cash himself: *"Nobody is in a position to sell machines, Class A and Class B, provide transactional equipment, the AFTs, the point of sales, to transact, and then to have cash. Unheard of."* He said he has friends who own banks.

**3. Georgia Class A is the big bet.** Val's read: Class B is capped, taxed at ~20% and restricted per location; Class A is none of those things and has no machine limit. He met the Georgia Lottery (GLC) last week, asked for regulations and was told there are none — *"so it's the wild west."* His conclusion is that the large manufacturers cannot move (18–24 months to a risk-capital decision), which leaves the field to fast, aggressive companies. He predicts the Georgia market goes from **47,000 machines to 150,000**. He also said that if legislation does come, he knows how to delay it.

**4. Pricing: do not discount, extend terms instead.** A worked argument from Val. Operators in this market buy on cash flow, not price — so terms matter more than price. Better to hold a high price and extend terms than to cut the price; and if a discount becomes unavoidable, discount from a high price, or add value (a POS, a kiosk, a store security package) rather than lower the number. He wants a Class B machine that is **more expensive and looks expensive**; Class A is the opposite — mass market, fight for every ten dollars. The games lead pushed back on this, arguing JVL is well positioned as the *economical* alternative to Aristocrat, Light & Wonder and IGT, and that nobody pulls an IGT out to put a JVL in. Val's answer: nobody needs to — there are about six machines per location and the first three being taken does not block the rest; and what actually sells into this market is a **new shape of machine**, not better game design.

**5. Single-title versus multi-game, and content cadence.** Val floated making Class A a **single-title machine with a cell modem**, so a weak game can be replaced remotely, and argued five single titles in a row read better than five identical multi-game boxes. The games lead countered that multi-game is "the religion" here and that players expect it. On Class B Val wants **more than eight, closer to ten** games, plus **three different progressive types** — which he called an accident they stumbled onto and the games lead said was entirely intentional (he redesigned the set after Felipe told him the games were all too similar). Val then proposed introducing **two new games every two weeks or every month**, always replacing the lowest earner, as an operator-facing pitch. The games lead warned this is what broke WMS — churn, dribbler games, players never settling. Val agreed on the risk but said he is not proposing second-class games, and noted that unlike the casino world there is no GLI submission here (*"each game through GLI is seven and a half thousand dollars"*), so content can move fast.

**6. Player-experience friction — a concrete idea.** The games lead described watching a cash-out fail at a location the night before: the ticket did not work in the dispenser, the barcode did not print fully, the attendant had no idea what to do. His proposal: on cash-out, hold the machine on the same game at the same denomination for a **30-second grace period**, so a player who re-inserts the ticket lands back where they were. His reasoning — breaking the cadence breaks the session. He is also working on JVL's own version of a streak feature (currently called "party time"), with graphics from Lena.

**7. Street-cabinet hardware review (01:08 onwards, in person).** Val inspected a machine with two other people and was unhappy with the build: wires must run from the back, not hang; the holder needs a slot, a pem and a limiter; and a screen is currently **glued** on, which he rejected outright — *"glueing it is not a solution... you gotta fix that."* He asked for it to be photographed and documented. On the spec generally: strip GLI-driven cost, use cell modem and a bulk data plan instead of expensive memory so games can be loaded overnight, front-mounted focused speaker (he says the current audio is "assassinated"), AFT standard with **no bill acceptor and no printer**, optional back-load validator at a third of the price, and no option on the vertical screen.

**8. Competitor read.** Val on **Ark**: their interim president was double-dipping, running his own kiosk company on the side, pre-sold and got paid for 170 kiosks, and owns no engineering — he subcontracts it. Val's argument against subcontracted engineering is that capabilities drift from a moving market, and he applied it to JVL too: of JVL's own first set of transactional capabilities, *"at least fifty percent of those capabilities are dead"* and some of what is about to ship is already obsolete. On **Primero**: genuine respect for how they sniff out poorly regulated niches and make money, but "naturally sloppy" engineering — preassembled monitors hung together rather than specified.

**9. Staffing and rollout.** Val expects **November**, not October, to be the swamped month for installations, hoping for Wi-Fi configuration by then so twenty units can go out at a time. Andrei is coming back, Sebastian will be there, and Val is moving a technician from Nebraska (quiet, smart, also named Andre) because Nebraska is slow.
