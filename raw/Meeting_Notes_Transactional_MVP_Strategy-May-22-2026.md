**Three-pronged MVP strategy.** Val laid out his current view on JVL's market entry: the spider web spins out from three products — **Flex IQ, Operator's Dashboard, and Tito Cloud Server**. Juan added Flex Card Dispenser for phase one (POS needs to output the card); Val agreed but said dispenser ships shortly after, not in phase one. Together, the IQ + dispenser + dashboard combination dramatically reduces fraud and simplifies operations. Even when an operator says "I don't care, I have no fraud" or "I know everything myself," Val believes they're wrong — and a flawlessly built, "Apple-ish" minimal-viable IQ + dashboard is a powerful entry point. The discipline is "taking our time now and doing it right."

**IQ functionality details.** IQ sits between the slot machine and the printer, intercepting the Moo the Goose ticket cash-out signal. The attendant validates and activates the gift card on IQ; if the customer wants a receipt, the attendant presses a button on IQ, and IQ commands the existing Moo the Goose thermal printer to print the receipt on its existing thermal roll paper. No new printer is needed. Currently no receipt prints from Moo the Goose alone — the ticket is the only physical artifact. IQ has two screens (one customer-facing, one attendant-facing) plus a hidden internal connector for a third remote screen, used in Georgia locations where protection glass separates the attendant from customer area so the customer-facing screen needs to be mirrored on a remote unit outside the glass.

**Operator dashboard design philosophy.** Val argued the dashboard should ship as a "skeleton" first — show the operator the full layout of everything that will eventually populate it, even if fields are empty, then add real features weekly. Showing operators the dashboard "epicenter" with all future fields visible will make them "salivate" — they're starved for information today and even a wireframe with the right structure will sell. Andrei pointed at the HTML draft he shared the prior week; Val confirmed Andrei's experience with dashboards far exceeds his own and deferred to Andrei as the expert. Juan committed to producing sample reports over the weekend showing two phases: phase one (without SMIB or Flexport — Moo the Goose data only, plus card transaction data from Incom samples) and phase two (with SMIB + port, more elaborate reporting). Juan noted "of all the dashboards I've seen in my life, they all kind of sucked" — his approach has historically been to export the data into SQL and build the reports he actually wants to see, not what the system vendor wants him to see.

**What the IQ-only dashboard can show.** Andrei walked through what a minimal IQ + dashboard product (no SMIB, no Flexport) could provide: everything Moo the Goose outputs (Moo the Goose is the existing accounting/data source), plus IQ service-state health, plus gift card issuance details (card IDs, values, dates) — though Val clarified that gift card detail does not live in IQ itself; IQ issues the card and pushes everything to the Payment Hub, JVL's in-house service which interfaces to Microsoft Business Central. Payment Hub is a hub, not an accounting station — it doesn't issue invoices or reconcile accounts. A separate, detailed conversation about which accounting data flows where was deferred to next week (Tuesday May 26 at 10 AM with Juan + Nathan + Andrei).

**Texas/Nebraska market context.** Andrei asked whether IQ + dashboard appeals to Texas and Nebraska markets given those states don't use gift cards the same way Georgia does. Juan explained: Nebraska uses the same Intralot system as Georgia but the Nebraska Department of Revenue chose not to give manufacturers any operator reports, so operators there are starved for data. Texas has no regulator and no reporting obligation at all — operators know cash collected but not losses, machine overpayments, or machine performance. Both markets are "dying for information." Gift cards are not prohibited in either state — they're available everywhere in pharmacies, Walgreens, CVS, and Texas can absolutely use them; Nebraska's regs don't appear to block them either. The pitch in these markets is cost savings: switching from cash to cards saves operators on refill labor, kiosk maintenance, and cash exposure, and a new Texas location can launch card-only without ever introducing cash.

**Sales doctrine.** Val laid out his sales philosophy in detail: don't come into a sales visit with a pre-programmed presentation; come in saying "we have a transactional product family — what are your pain points?" Operators love talking about their pain points; the entire conversation should be the operator describing problems and the salesperson identifying which JVL product fits. Coming in with "you need an IQ" can fail because it may require operational-model change the operator hasn't bought into yet. Andrei asked about the Texas expo flyer (transactional family overview, no prices, no specific solutions); Val approved it — it's a family-of-products introduction, not a presentation. Val was emphatic: "If I see you coming in with a PowerPoint presentation, I'm going to fire you. I hate those fucking things." Be yourself, be a normal individual, be either beef or pork, not both.

**PrizePay debit-card cash conversion.** Andrei asked about the PrizePay card video Felipe shared. Val confirmed PrizePay is Incom's reloadable second card, redeemable at ATM with a service fee (the video showed cash-out at ATM with a ~$5 fee). Juan flagged it sounded illegal — converting gift cards directly to cash crosses the line that distinguishes skill games from casinos, which Mike Patel (GOMOA president) is challenging because the industry doesn't want federal heat. Val explained the operator in the video can do it because he owns his own ATM and uses a non-Incom processor, so he's outside the regulated chain — and the regulator publicly stated they don't enforce or mandate anything at the kiosk, only at the machine.

**US legal/cultural framework (extended).** Val took an extended detour to explain US law to Andrei, who comes from a unitary-law paradigm: the US is a constitutional republic, not a federal-law-from-the-top system. There is federal law, state law, multiple legislators, interest groups, and a court system that ultimately interprets the constitution — which allows the pendulum to swing far in many directions over long stretches. "In the US, laws are recommendations" (Juan added); "as long as you pay your taxes" (Val). Val illustrated this with two examples: (1) Canadian gambling law was unwritten until 1996 because the 1935 criminal code didn't define screens or slot machines, "and we made a shitload of money"; (2) Seminole tribal gaming started in tents and tiki huts at the Seminole Classic in 1978 with 300 pull-tab machines — federally illegal but legal on tribal land — and grew to $2.5B/year by the time Juan left as VP of operations. Tribes are foreign governments inside the US not subject to local laws. The lesson: Mike Patel can protest skill-games-to-cash conversion all he wants, but the loophole is clever, it generates state tax revenue (Georgia gets $1 of every $5 service fee), and operators will lobby, fund politicians, and pay for new legislation if anyone threatens it. The pendulum will swing where the money is.

**"Cash is king" thesis.** Val: "Fuck the automation. Whatever you want, you could draw all the automation in the world… but cash is king." The reason IQ matters on the counter: the moment a card is issued, the player will turn it into cash — either at an ATM (paying ~$5 ATM fee on top of the ~$2 card fee) or via the attendant who can offer to buy back the card at $48 for $50 face value (cheaper than ATM, builds relationship, keeps the player at the machine because they don't have to leave to cash out, and the attendant earns the spread). Tito and e-wallet are valuable precisely because they prevent cash-out events — Moo the Goose forces a cash-out (you get a ticket; what do you do with a ticket?), which is "the worst event in a casino's life" and which structurally creates the conditions for illegal cash-redemption activity. By contrast, buying a debit card with value is legal — and that legalizes the entire transaction chain.

**Pricing vs Pax positioning.** A standard question Andrei should be ready to face from operators: Pax costs $300, JVL IQ will sell for $1,000 — why pay 3x? Val's answer: Pax enables theft (cash transaction on a register isn't equivalent — it's "another way of stealing money"). IQ + dashboard provides theft prevention, account reconciliation (which Pax doesn't), and the path to SMIB ($10/machine) + Flexport (~$1,000 retail) integration that eventually replaces Moo the Goose entirely. The upsell sequence: IQ first → dashboard → SMIB ("with every IQ I'll give you 6 free SMIBs") → Flexport → eventually player loyalty cards, promotions, mail-out card validation at location. Marketing levers include "JVL dollars" — turn part of port cost into JVL store credit redeemable for spare parts, machines, IQ units, more ports.

**Operator-vs-location dynamic.** Val explained the structural conflict: operators and locations distrust each other. Locations view operators as "stupid idiots making money in spite of themselves"; operators view locations as "extortionists" simply because they own gas stations. Operators want fast bank-account transfer and want the location's role minimized; locations want to keep cash exposure and personal-player relationships. This dynamic plays especially well in Nebraska and Texas because operators in those states are trying to own their own locations (similar to Pennsylvania).

**Trusted-player relationship preservation.** Andrei initially proposed a fully-automated IQ variant that excludes the attendant — customer scans Tito ticket, takes a card from a dispenser, swipes it himself, card activates. Juan pointed out this breaks the trusted human-to-human relationship operators specifically said they want to preserve in Georgia (an attendant can offer the player "sell me your $50 card for $45" — only possible with two humans). Val: it depends on the market — spielhalle locations in Germany are fully automated, that works in Germany; what works in Georgia may not work in Germany. JVL stays adaptive (fully automated / fully attended / hybrid) based on operator preference, but the immediate priority is breaking as few cycles as possible — Moo the Goose has been installed in ~6,500 locations for 20+ years at 75% market share, and operators won't burn hundreds of millions to switch unless forced.

**Editing the cash-out value.** Andrei asked: if IQ receives a $50 cash-out signal from the machine, can the attendant edit that value to support trusted-player negotiations? Juan confirmed yes — that editability is part of the design and one of the additional security advantages.
