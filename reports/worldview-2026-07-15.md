## The world in one page

¶1. JVL is a slot-machine company run by its owner **Val Levitan**, mid-way through remaking itself. The corporate skeleton: **JVL Labs** is the parent (Inna Levitan ~91%, with small passive holders); **JVL Systems** (US/Florida) and **JVL Gameworks** are wholly-owned operating entities, alongside Colombia, a closing Mexico, and a 95/5 Peru operation. The address of record for the whole group is now the Aventura Industrious office.

¶2. The business has two engines running at once. The **land-based core** — physical cabinets (the Flex family), sold and retrofitted into route markets — still pays the bills: Georgia's COAM market is the anchor, with South Dakota, Nebraska, and Puerto Rico as expansion fronts. The **new bet** is a payments ecosystem, **FlexPay/FlexPort**, meant to let players cash out at machines and stores where cash-payout is being legislated away.

¶3. Underneath both sits a deliberate **engineering transformation**: migrating off Bitbucket/SVN to GitHub, restructuring Jira around "value streams" and a new "capability" layer, consolidating games onto the **Omni** framework with the **Miranda** authoring tool, running online games through **RGS**, and adopting AI tooling aggressively — including the transcript-knowledge-base tool that is the direct ancestor of this very second brain.

## The most central people

¶4. **Val Levitan** is the gravitational center — he sets product direction, sits in nearly every meeting, and rules on architecture, hiring, pricing, and design personally. His authority is near-absolute; the recurring risk is bus-factor and over-centralization, which his own Constitution names.

## State of play at ~April 18, 2025

¶8. **FlexPay is the hot front.** The team settled a two-device architecture (FlexSMIB for modern machines, FlexPort-with-screen for legacy), chose SAS 603 over G2S, and is racing hardware — a "dispensing tower," magnetic-stripe-only cards, an InComm gift-card rail (Sutton Bank custody), and a July-2026 legislative deadline driving urgency. But Val has *already* begun second-guessing the kiosk-centric model ("FlexPay Evolution"), fearing cost-averse Georgia operators (Mike Patel's pulse-capture objection) won't buy.

¶9. **Games/Omni have momentum with slippage.** Kai-Ocho is the first Omni-framework game, targeted at GLI by June 1; the math has slipped repeatedly. Georgia's Peach releases and Diamond Link V-Deck work continue. **Markets:** South Dakota has a VLT licence in hand, Nebraska is applied-for, Puerto Rico's law firm (Estrella/Carlos Infante) is engaged, and **Mexico is being wound down hard** — compressed from six months to ~30 days.

¶10. **Engineering doctrine is crystallizing:** repository = product; feature branches ≤ 1 week; sprints stretched to ~3–4 weeks; "no test, no pull"; testers replaced by Test Engineers; a six-product GitHub re-carve; secrets on Azure. The Business Central ERP rollout and a software-licensed-per-operator (not per-machine) model round out the back office.

## Through-lines

¶11. **"If it isn't recorded, it wasn't decided."** Val's operating philosophy — one source of truth, produce only what's necessary, AI drafts / people decide, traceability from requirement to code — runs through nearly every meeting and is codified in the JVL Constitution.

¶12. **Legislative pressure as product strategy.** Nearly every market move (FlexPay, Georgia COAM kits, Class-II bingo, Puerto Rico) is a response to gaming law — what counts as skill vs gambling, how players may be paid, which lab certifies. JVL's edge is reading and racing regulation.

¶13. **Reuse over reinvention.** "Recycle the math and mechanics, not the look"; reimagine legacy titles; retrofit old cabinets with kits; buy reliable tools rather than build. Val repeatedly frames spend as leverage, not cost.

## What changed as the picture filled in

¶14. Early on the online-games business looked like a real growth arm; by the Gottlieb assessment it reads as structurally hard ("providers own the casinos"), and Val's counter is a "Netflix for games" independent platform — aspirational, not committed. The SMIB architecture flipped mid-stream (display "not necessary" → legacy gets a screen). The CMS plan moved from "reorganize by features" to "as-is transfer, gated by a five-day rule." The Gameworks address churned through a full ruling-and-reversal before settling on 19505 Biscayne.
