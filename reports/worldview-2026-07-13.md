# The world as this second brain currently sees it

*Worldview essay #1 — written 2026-07-13 from the wiki pages only (ingested timeline: Jan 2 – Mar 11, 2025; ~7% of the backlog). An owner comprehension check, not a wiki page. Paragraphs are numbered for reference.*

¶1 — JVL is a group of companies that designs and builds gambling machines — "video lottery terminals" (VLTs) and amusement/gaming cabinets — and, increasingly, the software and online games that run on and around them. The wiki records at least three legal entities (JVL Systems in the US/Florida, JVL Labs in Canada, a Gameworks arm), an engineering team concentrated in Minsk, a warehouse operation in Mexico, and sales into several US route markets plus Latin America. You, Val Levitan, are the principal decision-maker in every meeting; your wife Ina is recorded as the 91% owner.

¶2 — The machines are sold or placed into "route" markets — bars, stores, gas stations, small locations — rather than big casinos, with Georgia as the core established co-op market and Florida, South Dakota, and Puerto Rico as expansion fronts. Alongside the physical business there is an online-casino games business (the "RGS" — remote game server) that distributes JVL's game math to online operators, and a brand-new consumer product, ECHO, a home gaming device.

¶3 — Reading across the pages, the thing you appear to be trying to accomplish (interpretation) is to modernize a hand-built engineering shop into a disciplined software company — Git, automated pipelines, AI tooling, proper documentation — while simultaneously pushing hard into new markets and new product lines, all under constant cost pressure. The tension between "ship the next market now" and "rebuild how we work" runs through almost everything.

¶4 — The most central person after you is **Aaron (Bockelie)**, who functions as an outside software architect/consultant driving the entire engineering modernization: the move off Jenkins to a Git-native build pipeline, GitHub adoption and access model, AI-assisted development, docs-as-code, and the plan to retire Atlassian. You explicitly note he should be used as a consultant, not an employee. He coaches your people relentlessly; the wiki reads as you and Aaron jointly re-educating the Minsk team.

¶5 — On the commercial/field side, **Juan Martinez** is your right hand: GLI dealings, the South Dakota and Puerto Rico expansions, game creative direction, and JIRA product requests. **Pavel (Frolov)** is your engineering director — the build pipeline (feature "DTP-53"), drivers, printer support, and the FlexPort/FlexPay payment hardware. **Genadi Peari** is the hardware lead (the FP7 CPU board, cabling, printers).

¶6 — The Minsk engineering bench is deep and, in the transcripts, easy to confuse: **Efim Podvoiski** owns the RGS and its game math (he guards the "math container"); **Lena Zhukova** leads game design/art; **Dmitry Khandov** does design/video and tooling; **Andrei Bosik** is being groomed to own DevSecOps and the GitHub migration; **Alexander Biratka** ("Sasha") runs hardware/performance tests and pipeline work. **Michael** is a game producer working closely with Juan on slot design.

¶7 — A distinct commercial figure is **Andrei Klimovich**, who runs the online-games/RGS business and the ECHO Amazon push — deliberately kept separate in the wiki from Andrei Bosik the engineer. The relationship texture the wiki captures best is you as a forceful, non-technical chairman "throwing ideas at" engineers to extract information; one recorded exchange with Efim over opening up the RGS was openly tense.

¶8 — Outside JVL, the recurring external players are **GLI** (the game-certification lab that gates every release), **Elevate** (a specialty lender), **Incom** (a payment processor for the Georgia prize-card kiosk), **CIBC** (US banking), and freight vendors. Per the owner ruling recorded on the Elevate page, Elevate is a **lender** that took a pledge on the machines financing a Colombian operator — not a customer.

¶9 — State of play as of mid-March 2025. The **software modernization** has real momentum: the Git-native build system, GitHub-over-Bitbucket, private-by-default repos, a three-layer continuous-delivery architecture, and a staged repo migration are all decided and underway, with the graphics team being onboarded to Git LFS. This is the most thoroughly documented part of the whole wiki.

¶10 — **Hardware** reached a settled but constrained place: after testing, the FP7 board is CPU-bound, so the decision was to optimize rather than upgrade, with a more powerful 8000-processor board promised around March 1 and the Omni game engine upgraded to Pixi 8.6.

¶11 — The **market rollouts** are the pressured front. Georgia (your core revenue) is being released in four staged "Peach" packages, under an explicit warning from you that you expect a ~40% revenue drop there because two competitors entered — to the point of naming payroll as at risk. South Dakota is progressing through lab testing (the Epic 950 printer, release "SDVLT 1.11.5") but slipped from a February to an early-March target. Puerto Rico is in the licensing-outreach stage as the Peru office closes.

¶12 — The **payment products** (FlexPort hardware ↔ FlexPay kiosk, with Incom) are in active engineering bring-up: as of March 11 the integration is near feature-complete, with a two-machine office test planned and firmware-update and bill-acceptor cabling still pending.

¶13 — On the **product/design** side, a firm decision emerged that games must be signed off on final assembled machines shipped from Georgia (triggered by a "Kai-Ocho" stutter bug), and there is an active, still-open debate about whether progressives should scale by denomination.

¶14 — The through-lines that recur month after month: **GLI certification** as the master bottleneck on cost and speed (it gates everything and is expensive); **money pressure** (the Georgia revenue warning, cost-conscious tool decisions, the "no test tool this year" ruling); **regulators everywhere** (GLI, state gaming boards, the Puerto Rico Gaming Commission); and the **engineering-discipline crusade** — Git, "the only source of truth is what's in git," AI tooling, and killing tribal knowledge.

¶15 — A softer through-line is your management style itself: the wiki is full of you coaching, pushing, and occasionally overruling, with Aaron as the co-teacher. Persona-driven design, feature-branch discipline, and JIRA hygiene all show up as things you are trying to instill, not things that already exist.

¶16 — A commercial through-line is diversification away from dependence on any one market: land-based route markets, online RGS distribution, and the ECHO home device are three distinct bets, with you naming ECHO a near-term priority and insisting online games be built fresh from land-based mechanics rather than ported.

¶17 — What changed across the period. The **Elevate relationship was re-understood**: an early (Jan 14) framing of Elevate as a customer financed through EDC was corrected by a Jan 15 call and, on your ruling, recorded as a lender that took a pledge — the EDC role now uncertain. This is the clearest example of the wiki revising an early belief.

¶18 — The **hardware story also flipped**: FP7 was first simply "adopted," then, after bottleneck testing, reframed as a ceiling to optimize against rather than a win. And a large amount of early ambiguity was **name/identity resolution** — several people were initially recorded under garbled or partial names (now corrected to Biratka, Efim Podvoiski, Genadi Peari, Juan Martinez, Felipe Miranda) and several look-alike names were split into distinct people (two Antons, two Andreis, two Khandovs).

¶19 — **Honest uncertainty.** The largest caveat dwarfs the rest: this picture is only Jan 2 – Mar 11, 2025 — under 7% of the material — so everything above is the opening chapters, not the book. Emphasis will shift as the remaining ~900 transcripts land; anything called "central" here is central *so far*.

¶20 — Coverage is uneven. The **software/engineering** world is richly documented; the **money and corporate structure** are thin — the wiki asserts entities and an ownership split but has almost no real financial picture, and the exact relationships among the JVL entities are barely sketched. The **online-games commercial** side (Andrei Klimovich, ECHO, the "Marketplace") is thinner than its apparent importance, resting on a handful of recap meetings.

¶21 — Several things look important but are underdocumented or unresolved. A recurring **"Dima"** appears across GitHub-admin, IT-structure, observability, and embedded contexts and has deliberately *not* been merged into any one person — the wiki genuinely does not know how many Dimas there are. Whether **Michael the game producer is the same person as "Misha"** the retrofit lead is open. The **FlexPay mobile app** (as opposed to the FlexPay payment hardware) is single-source and flagged as unconfirmed. One personal/tax matter was deliberately excluded as out of scope.

¶22 — The five questions the wiki would most want answered to sharpen the picture: (1) Who are the distinct "Dima"s, and is any of them Dmitry Khandov? (2) What is the actual money flow — who funds what, and what are JVL's real revenues and obligations? (3) How do the JVL entities (Systems, Labs, Gameworks) actually divide the business? (4) Is Michael = Misha, and more broadly who are the ~half-dozen still-single-source people who keep recurring? (5) Is the FlexPay *mobile app* a live initiative or an early idea that faded? Answering these would move the wiki from "understands JVL's engineering" toward "understands JVL."
